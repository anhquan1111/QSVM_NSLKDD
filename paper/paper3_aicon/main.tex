\begin{abstract}
% TODO(tac gia thu nhat): viet lai cho gon, 150-200 tu. Khung lap luan:
% (1) danh gia mo hinh luong tu tren du lieu lech lop co mot cai bay;
% (2) day la mot ca that, phat hien va bac bo bang chinh du lieu cua nhom;

% (4) cai bay khong dac thu cho kernel luong tu -- bang kiem dem cho thay the;
% (5) cong cu de bat, phat hanh kem.
Selecting a hyper-parameter by cross-validated $\Fone$ on class-imbalanced
data can return a model that classifies every record into one class. We
document a case in which this happened to a four-qubit quantum-kernel
intrusion detector on UNSW-NB15, produced an apparent robustness result, and
was refuted only when the regularisation constant was held fixed. We show the
mechanism and show, by census, that it is not specific to the quantum kernel.
We then measure what would have prevented it: on a denser grid the collapse
does not survive selection under any of three objectives, but for one
classical kernel every objective still returns a degenerate constant, so a
class-averaged objective is a mitigation rather than a guarantee. The one
check that identifies every case is the count of true negatives, and we
release the checks that implement it.
\keywords{Quantum kernel \and Model selection \and Evaluation protocol \and
Intrusion detection \and Reproducibility}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% TODO(tac gia thu nhat): doc lai, nới rong neu con cho.
Quantum-kernel methods reached network intrusion detection quickly after
quantum feature maps were proposed as a route to expressive
kernels~\cite{havlicek2019,schuld2019hilbert} and separations were
established for constructed learning problems~\cite{liu2021speedup}; the
applied literature now reports them on standard IDS
corpora~\cite{payares2021dos,kalinin2023qids}. Asking what such methods
actually deliver presumes that the measurement itself is sound. This paper is about a case where it was not,
and where the failure was invisible in every number the protocol reported.

Evaluating a kernel classifier on intrusion-detection data means choosing a
regularisation constant, and the standard way to choose it is to maximise a
cross-validated score. On class-imbalanced data that procedure has a trap.
The constant classifier that calls everything an attack attains a
respectable binary $\Fone$, and that value acts as a floor beneath the whole
grid. A model whose genuine scores never rise above the floor will have the
degenerate setting returned to it by $\arg\max$, and will then be carried
through the rest of the study as though it were a working model.

The selected model predicted a single class for every
test record. It went on to produce a robustness result --- $\Fone$ invariant
under input perturbation --- that was an artefact of the prediction being
constant, and that result was not obviously wrong on inspection. It was
caught only when the regularisation constant was fixed at a neutral value
and the experiment re-run.

We report the case, the mechanism behind it, and a census showing that the
collapse is a property of the interaction between the regularisation
constant and the selection objective rather than of the quantum kernel. The
contribution is not a better model; it is a caution about a procedure that
most papers in this area run without reporting, and the checks that detect
it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A documented case in which $\arg\max$ selection over a
        cross-validated $\Fone$ grid returned a degenerate classifier, and the
        degenerate classifier then produced an apparent robustness result.
  \item The mechanism: on imbalanced folds the constant all-attack classifier
        attains $\Fone = \pdegFloor$, a floor that no grid point for the
        quantum kernel exceeded.
  \item A census showing the collapse is a property of the
        regularisation--objective interaction rather than of the quantum
        kernel (Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy}).
  \item A measurement, not a conjecture, of what would have prevented it:
        a denser grid removes the collapse for the quantum kernel under
        every objective tested, while a class-averaged objective leaves it
        in place for a classical kernel (Table~\ref{tab:objective}). The
        two standard remedies are therefore partial, and the quantity that
        separates every case is the count of true negatives.
  \item The audit checks that detect it, released with the paper.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Three lines of work meet here, and the gap this paper reports sits between
them rather than inside any one of them.

\paragraph{Quantum kernels applied to intrusion detection.}
Quantum feature maps were proposed as a route to kernels that are hard to
evaluate classically~\cite{havlicek2019,schuld2019hilbert}, and a separation
was later established for a constructed learning
problem~\cite{liu2021speedup}. The applied literature carried the idea to
standard IDS corpora quickly: Payares and
Mart\'{\i}nez-Santos~\cite{payares2021dos} compare quantum and classical
models on denial-of-service detection, and Kalinin and
Krundyshev~\cite{kalinin2023qids} survey quantum techniques for intrusion
detection. Both report aggregate scores --- accuracy, precision, recall,
$\Fone$ --- and neither reports a confusion matrix or any statement that the
compared classifiers predict more than one class. Nothing in either paper
suggests it suffered the failure reported here. The point is narrower and it
concerns the reader: from what is printed, the failure would not be
distinguishable from a working model.

\paragraph{Metrics that conceal a degenerate classifier.}
That binary $\Fone$ flatters a classifier on imbalanced data is known.
Chicco and Jurman~\cite{chicco2020mcc} show that $\Fone$ and accuracy return
inflated scores on imbalanced problems precisely because neither reads all
four cells of the confusion matrix, and argue for a coefficient that does.
Christen \emph{et al.}~\cite{christen2023fmeasure} gather the criticism of
the F-measure and its alternatives in one place. In computer security the
point is catalogued directly: Arp \emph{et al.}~\cite{arp2022mlsec} name
\emph{inappropriate performance measures} and the \emph{base rate fallacy}
among ten recurring pitfalls --- the first being measures that ignore
constraints such as imbalanced data, the second being imbalance ignored when
results are interpreted.

What this paper adds is where the measure sits. That literature treats the
choice as a \emph{reporting} problem: the wrong number is printed about a
model that exists independently of it. Here the same measure is the
\emph{selection} objective, so it does not misdescribe a model --- it
chooses one, and every number computed downstream describes a classifier
that predicts a single class. A reporting error can be repaired afterwards
by recomputing a metric on stored predictions. This one cannot, because the
artefact is the model.

\paragraph{Evaluation blind spots that outlived a literature.}
The nearest precedent is not an intrusion-detection result at all. Guo
\emph{et al.}~\cite{guo2017calibration} showed that modern classifiers are
systematically over-confident, and that the effect grows with capacity --- a
defect invisible in accuracy, which was the quantity the field reported, and
obvious the moment a second quantity was plotted. The post-hoc corrections
that followed~\cite{platt1999svm} presume that diagnosis has been made. The
present case has the same shape at smaller scale: one reported number, and
one unreported number that decides whether the first means anything.

\paragraph{The constant $C$ and the degenerate solution.}
The soft-margin formulation~\cite{cortes1995svm} trades margin width against
hinge loss. As $C$ falls the margin term dominates, and on a class-imbalanced
sample the minimiser approaches the constant classifier that predicts the
majority class --- which on an attack-heavy split is the attack class. That
much is standard and is not a defect of the solver. What this paper adds is
that a selection objective can \emph{prefer} that solution over every
non-degenerate alternative on the grid, and that whether it does turns on a
choice of averaging usually made without comment.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

Both protocols use the same front end on UNSW-NB15: one-hot encoding,
\textsc{SelectKBest}, PCA, and a min--max map onto $[0,\pi]$, every
transformer fitted on the training subset alone. The quantum kernel is the
\ZZ{}-FeatureMap with full entanglement, evaluated as an exact statevector
fidelity. Classical kernels are linear, degree-two polynomial, and RBF, all
fitted by the same soft-margin solver~\cite{cortes1995svm} so that the only difference between
arms is the kernel and the regularisation constant.

The evidence below comes from two protocols that must not be conflated.
The \emph{tuned} protocol uses \plegacyQubits{} qubits, $N_{\text{train}} =
N_{\text{test}} = \plegacyN$ and \plegacySeeds{} seeds, with $C$ chosen by
$\arg\max$ over a \pcvFolds-fold cross-validated $\Fone$ on the grid
$\{\pcGrid\}$. The \emph{revision} protocol uses \prevQubits{} qubits,
training sizes up to $\prevMaxN$ and \prevRuns{} runs. Every claim below states which
protocol it belongs to.

\section{The trap}\label{sec:trap}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig1_tuning_trap.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Cross-validated $\Fone$ against $C$ for four kernels. The dashed
  line is the $\Fone$ of the constant all-attack classifier, $\pdegFloor$. All
  four kernels sit exactly on it at $C = \pselectedC$; the linear and
  polynomial kernels rise above it at $C = 0.1$ and the RBF kernel at $C = 1$,
  but the quantum kernel never does --- its best non-degenerate score is
  $\pqBestNonDeg$, \emph{below} the floor. Circles mark the value $\arg\max$
  returns. (b) What that selection costs on the test set.}
  \label{fig:trap}
\end{figure}

On the tuned protocol the grid search returned $C = \pselectedC$ for the
quantum kernel. The selected model has recall $\pdegRecall$ with standard
deviation $\pdegRecallStd$ across all \plegacySeeds{} runs, precision
$\pdegPrecision$ equal to the attack ratio of the test split, and
$\mathrm{TN} = \pdegTN$: it calls every test record an attack.

The mechanism is Fig.~\ref{fig:trap}(a). A constant all-attack classifier
attains $\Fone = \pdegFloorFull$ on these folds. That value is a floor, and
every kernel reaches it at $C = \pselectedC$. The linear and polynomial
kernels climb above the floor once $C \ge 0.1$, so $\arg\max$ moves away from
the degenerate point. The quantum kernel never climbs above it: its best
non-degenerate grid score is $\pqBestNonDeg$. Selection therefore had no
non-degenerate maximum to find, and returned the collapse.

Two features of that account deserve to be stated as what they are:
properties of the objective and of the grid, neither of which is a property
of the kernel.

The objective ignores the evidence. Binary $\Fone$ on the positive class
does not read true negatives at all, so a classifier that never predicts the
negative class loses nothing by it and is rewarded in proportion to the
positive rate. Macro-averaged $\Fone$ and balanced accuracy average over
classes and score the constant classifier near $0.5$. Whether that
difference is enough to prevent the collapse is a measurable question rather
than a rhetorical one, and Section~\ref{sec:catch} measures it. The answer
is only partly the expected one.

The grid is complicit. The claim above that the quantum kernel never climbs
above the floor holds for the grid that was searched, whose five points are
spaced a decade apart. Section~\ref{sec:catch} shows that the interval in
which this kernel does climb above the floor lies between two of those
points, so the collapse survived selection partly because nothing was
evaluated where the escape happens.

the prediction was constant --- against $\pperturbRangeNew$ once $C$ was held

\section{A degeneracy census}\label{sec:census}

\input{tables/degeneracy_census}

Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy} counts, for each protocol and model, the runs on
which the fitted classifier assigned every test record to the attack class.
Three points follow from it.

First, collapse is not a quantum phenomenon. Under the tuned protocol it
occurs for three of the four kernels, the linear and polynomial kernels
included. Under the revision protocol, whose model set also includes a random
forest~\cite{breiman2001rf} and gradient-boosted
trees~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, a scan of \prevRowsScanned{} rows
across \prevModels{} models finds \prevDegRows{} degenerate rows, all
belonging to \prevDegModel{}, and none belonging to the quantum kernel
(whose lowest macro recall anywhere in that scan is
\prevQsvmMinRecall).

Second, what is specific to the quantum kernel is not that it collapsed but
that it collapsed on \emph{every} run. That is what made the collapse
survive selection: with no grid point scoring above the degenerate floor,
$\arg\max$ had no non-degenerate maximum available to it.

Third, holding the constant at a neutral value removes the effect entirely
--- \pkSweepDeg{} degenerate runs out of \pkSweepRuns{} across seven
feature budgets --- which is the evidence that the kernel was never the
problem.

One point about provenance. The revision rows of
Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy} are recomputed from the runs the companion
study~\cite{companion} reports, but they count a quantity that study does
not report: it states macro recall per run and never identifies a run as
degenerate. The census is therefore a new measurement over existing runs,
not a restatement of a published one, and we say so rather than leave it to
be discovered.

\section{What would have caught it}\label{sec:catch}

Section~\ref{sec:trap} named two things that separate the run that collapsed
from one that would not have: the averaging in the selection objective, and
the grid over which it was maximised. Both were assertions. This section
measures them.

The measurement reuses the stored Gram matrices of the tuned protocol, so no
quantum circuit is evaluated again and the models compared are the same
models. We refit on the cached kernels at \pcurveNc{} values of $C$ spaced
logarithmically from \pcurveCmin{} to \pcurveCmax{} --- the original five
points are a subset --- on all \pcurveRuns{} runs, and we repeat the
cross-validated selection under three objectives. Before writing any new
number, the same code path reproduces three published ones: the
cross-validated $\Fone$ at the original five grid points agrees to
\preproCv{}, and the test-set means at the tuned and at the neutral $C$
agree to \preproTest{}. A pipeline that could not return the numbers already
in print would not be entitled to produce new ones.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig3_objective.pdf}
  \caption{(a) The quantum kernel across the collapse boundary, on the test
  set, averaged over \pcurveRuns{} runs. Inside the shaded band every run
  assigns all test records to the attack class. Binary $\Fone$ --- the
  quantity selection maximised --- moves by \pcurveFoneRise{} across the
  band edge; macro $\Fone$ moves by \pcurveMacroRise. (b) Degenerate runs
  against $C$ for all four kernels. The quantum kernel escapes between
  $0.1$ and $1$, where the original grid had no point.}
  \label{fig:objective}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{One objective is nearly blind.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:objective}(a) follows the quantum kernel from the degenerate
floor to the smallest $C$ at which no run collapses, $C = \pcurveEscapeC$.
Across that transition the classifier stops predicting a single class ---
the largest qualitative change it can undergo. Binary $\Fone$ records it as
\pcurveFoneRise{} (\pcurveFoneLo{} to \pcurveFoneHi). Macro $\Fone$ records
it as \pcurveMacroRise{} (\pcurveMacroLo{} to \pcurveMacroHi), about
\pcurveRiseRatio{} times larger for the same event. That ratio is the
quantitative content of the remark that binary $\Fone$ ignores true
negatives. It does not miss the collapse entirely; it under-reports it by
close to an order of magnitude, which is the more dangerous failure, because
a number that moves a little looks like a number that is working.

\paragraph{The grid mattered as much as the objective.}
\input{tables/objective_table}
Table~\ref{tab:objective} gives what $\arg\max$ returns on the denser grid.
For the quantum kernel \emph{every} objective now returns a non-degenerate
constant, including the binary $\Fone$ that failed originally: it returns
$C = \pobjQBinC$ with \pobjQBinDeg{} runs degenerate.
Fig.~\ref{fig:objective}(b) shows why. This kernel escapes between $0.1$ and
$1$, and the original grid stepped straight across that interval. The
collapse was therefore not the objective's doing alone; adding points where
the escape happens would have avoided it with the objective untouched.

\paragraph{A class-averaged objective is not a guarantee.}
The obvious remedy fails on one of the four kernels here. For the linear
kernel all three objectives return the same constant, $C = \pobjLinBinC$,
and it is degenerate on \pobjLinMacDeg{} runs. Class-averaged objectives do
lower the score of a constant classifier, but that is not the same as making
a degenerate solution unreachable: they change which model scores highest,
not whether the highest-scoring model predicts two classes. On the RBF
kernel the remedy does work --- binary $\Fone$ returns a constant degenerate
on \pobjRbfBinDeg{} runs, while both class-averaged objectives return
$C = \pobjRbfMacC{}$ with \pobjRbfMacDeg. The remedy is real and partial. A
study that adopted it and reported nothing further could still publish a
constant classifier.

What separates the rows that collapsed from the rows that did not is not the
objective. It is a single number that no aggregate score in
Table~\ref{tab:objective} displays: the count of true negatives.

\section{The checks}\label{sec:checks}

The fourth contribution is a set of checks, and they are worth describing
because they are small. Three of them run over this paper itself.

The first recomputes every number in the text from the stored artifacts.
Numbers reach the prose only through generated macros, and a separate scan
fails the build if a literal decimal appears in a sentence. This is what
makes the paper auditable rather than merely careful: a reader who doubts a
number can find the file it was computed from.

The second asserts the invariants the argument rests on, so that the
argument fails loudly if an artifact is regenerated and the conclusion no
longer follows from it. Two of them: on the original grid the quantum
kernel's best non-degenerate cross-validated score must lie \emph{below} the
degenerate floor, and each classical kernel's best score must lie above it.
If either reversed, Section~\ref{sec:trap} would be wrong, and the check
says so before a reader has to.

The third scans for degeneracy without filtering first. The census of
Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy} is computed over all \prevModels{} models and
\prevRowsScanned{} rows of the revision protocol, not over the models we
expected to find it in. That is what makes its negative result --- no
quantum-kernel row is degenerate there --- worth reporting at all.

None of this is specific to quantum kernels or to intrusion detection, and
the operational content is two lines in a reporting routine: record the
confusion matrix beside the score, and assert that each fitted model
predicts more than one class before any downstream result is computed. The
case in this paper would have been caught at the moment it was created.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The degeneracy evidence is a \plegacyQubits-qubit, $N = \plegacyN$,
\plegacySeeds-seed result. It has \emph{not} been reproduced under the
\prevQubits-qubit, ten-run revision protocol, whose symmetric tuning procedure
was adopted partly in response to this finding. The census in
Table~\ref{tab:degeneracy} therefore compares two protocols that differ in
more than one respect, and we draw no conclusion from that comparison beyond
the presence or absence of collapse.

The case is drawn from one corpus and one model family, and the degenerate
protocol used a small training and test split, so the census counts runs
rather than estimating a rate.

Section~\ref{sec:catch} carries its own limit. It measures what a denser
grid and two other objectives would have returned \emph{here} --- same
corpus, same \pcurveRuns{} runs, same $N = \plegacyN$ split, same cached
kernels. It therefore says what those choices would have done in this case,
and not what they do in general; the linear-kernel row is enough to show
that the general statement would have been false. We do not claim the trap
is common in the literature, only that it is easy to fall into and invisible
in the reported numbers when it happens.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

% TODO(tac gia thu nhat): doc lai truoc khi nop.
Selecting a regularisation constant by maximising a cross-validated binary
$\Fone$ can return a classifier that predicts one class for every input, and
the resulting model will pass through an evaluation pipeline producing
numbers that look like findings. We documented one such case, identified the
mechanism --- a degenerate floor in the selection objective that the model's
genuine scores never cleared --- and showed by census that the collapse
belongs to the regularisation--objective interaction rather than to the
quantum kernel.

We then measured what would have prevented it, rather than asserting it. On
a denser grid the collapse does not survive selection for the quantum kernel
under any of three objectives, including the binary $\Fone$ that failed
originally --- so the grid was as much at fault as the objective. But for
the linear kernel all three objectives still return a degenerate constant,
which makes a class-averaged objective a mitigation and not a guarantee.

One check identified every degenerate case in this paper, and it is the
cheapest of them: report the confusion matrix beside the score. A constant
predictor is then impossible to miss --- the same reflex that surfaced
systematic over-confidence in deep classifiers~\cite{guo2017calibration},
and that a recalibration step~\cite{platt1999svm} presumes has already been
taken. We release the checks that implement it.
