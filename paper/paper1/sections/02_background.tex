\subsection{Quantum kernels}

A quantum kernel embeds a classical vector $\bm{x}$ into a state
$|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle$ prepared by a parameterised circuit and reads off the
fidelity $|\langle\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime})|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle|^{2}$ as a
similarity \cite{havlicek2019,schuld2019}. The appeal is that the induced
feature space can be classically hard to compute while the kernel entries remain
cheap to \emph{estimate}, which is the basis of the proven separations for
carefully constructed problems. Whether that translates into an advantage on
natural tabular data is a separate empirical question, and it is the one this
paper addresses.

Two results bound expectations. Alignment-based bounds \cite{cristianini2002,%
cortes2012} say that a kernel better aligned with the labels admits a tighter
generalisation guarantee at equal empirical risk --- but they say nothing about
whether a given circuit produces such a kernel. In the other direction,
fidelity kernels from expressive maps concentrate exponentially toward a
constant as the number of qubits grows \cite{thanasilp2024}, so the same
expressivity that makes a map interesting makes its Gram matrix uninformative at
width. Sec.~\ref{sec:res-width} measures both effects in our setting: the
alignment gain is large and the concentration is what eventually destroys it.

\subsection{Quantum machine learning for intrusion detection}

Applications of quantum kernels and variational classifiers to network intrusion
detection have grown quickly, most using NSL-KDD \cite{ref4} or a successor
corpus \cite{ref25}; a 2026 survey \cite{kaissar2026} covers the field through
January of that year and reaches two conclusions that bear directly on this
paper: most published approaches rely on simulated quantum environments and
legacy datasets, and evaluation practice is inconsistent across studies. The
recurring pattern is a single operating point --- one qubit count, one
training-set size, one class prior --- with a reported accuracy compared against
a small set of classical baselines. Reported numbers vary
widely across studies that nominally use the same dataset, which is what one
would expect when preprocessing, subset size and baseline strength all differ
simultaneously.

The most directly comparable study is the benchmark of Cirillo \emph{et al.}
\cite{qmi2026}, which evaluates Pegasos-QSVC, a variational classifier and a
hybrid quantum--classical network on ToN\_IoT and NSL-KDD under ideal and noisy
IBM simulators, and reports Pegasos-QSVC best at $94.60\%$ accuracy. It varies the model family and the noise model; it does not vary the
embedding width or the training-set size, and it does not include a gradient-%
boosted tree baseline.

\subsection{Benchmarking studies of quantum models}

Two broad benchmarks are relevant precisely because they are sceptical. Bowles,
Ahmed and Schuld \cite{bowles2024} evaluate twelve QML models over $160$ datasets
in noise-free simulation and find that quantum models rarely beat well-tuned
classical baselines. Schnabel and Roth \cite{schnabel2024} run over $20{,}000$
tuned models comparing fidelity and projected quantum kernels across five
dataset families and analyse where the differences come from. Both conclude that
general-purpose feature maps seldom win once the classical comparison is done
properly, and our results agree with them at the aggregate level.

Concurrently with this revision, Gillani \emph{et al.} \cite{gillani2026}
benchmark quantum and classical models for intrusion detection across four
corpora, including both of ours, with FDR control over $108$ comparisons and an
attribution audit. Their conclusion --- that reported quantum gains are largely
attributable to the classical preprocessing front-end once the same front-end is
given to classical models --- corroborates, from a different direction, the
decomposition we report in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-c4}. We do not claim breadth over
that study: they evaluate four datasets to our two, at eight qubits to our four
and six. What they do not vary is the training-set size, and that is where our
contribution lies.

\subsection{Positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:novelty} places this work against those four studies on the axes
that distinguish them. The distinguishing axis is not the model, the dataset or
the noise treatment, on all of which we are comparable or narrower. It is that
none of these studies sweeps the training-set size, and the training-set size is
the variable that decides the answer here: the ordering between \QSVM{} and
every strong classical baseline reverses inside the range $N\in[10^{2},10^{4}]$
under the natural class prior, so a benchmark conducted at a single $N$ --- as
all of them are, and as our own submitted version was --- can report either
outcome and be internally consistent.

We take the reviewers' point that benchmarking alone is not a contribution, and
we note that the two studies most often cited as establishing what is already
known \cite{bowles2024,schnabel2024} are themselves benchmarks without a new
kernel, feature map, algorithm, hardware demonstration or theoretical result.
What makes a benchmark worth publishing is not its novelty of apparatus but
whether it changes what the field believes. We report an ordering that reverses
with sample size, a selection rule whose output transfers across datasets, and a
measured mechanism --- Gram-matrix concentration --- that predicts where the
method stops working. The last of these is the closest we come to a theoretical
contribution, and we present it as an empirical regularity rather than as a
theorem, which is what our evidence supports.
