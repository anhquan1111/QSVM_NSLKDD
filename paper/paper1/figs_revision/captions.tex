\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig5_c1_dimension_selection.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{The three-stage dimension-selection rule, applied unchanged to two
    datasets.}
    Rows: NSL-KDD (top) and UNSW-NB15 (bottom). Columns: (a) explained variance
    $V(n)$, (b) kernel-target alignment, (c) CNOT-weighted hardware cost $Q(n)$.
    The rule is applied in a fixed order and has no tunable weights: stage~1
    discards every $n$ with $V(n)<T=0.85$ (shaded); stage~2 keeps those with
    $\mathrm{KTA}(n)\ge 0.95\,\mathrm{KTA}_{\max}$ among the survivors (open
    circles); stage~3 selects the cheapest survivor (filled marker).
    The same procedure, with no parameter changed, returns $n^\ast=4$ on
    NSL-KDD and $n^\ast=6$ on UNSW-NB15, and $n^\ast=6$ is recovered on
    $10/10$ independent UNSW subsets and under every threshold perturbation we
    tested. The selected dimension is therefore a \emph{property of the dataset
    produced by a transferable procedure}, not a constant carried over from one
    experiment. Bootstrap intervals ($B=200$) are available for NSL-KDD only.
    Note that stage~1 is what removes $n=2$ on NSL-KDD despite its high
    alignment: a kernel can be well aligned on a representation that has
    already discarded most of the signal.}
  \label{fig:c1-selection}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig9_learning_curve_nslkdd.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{Rare-attack enrichment, not sample size alone, decides whether the
    quantum kernel leads on NSL-KDD.}
    (a,~b) Mean macro-$F_1$ over 10 runs on the full KDDTest+ set, with 95\%
    confidence bands on the highlighted series; (c,~d) the corresponding paired
    differences $\Delta F_1=$ QSVM-ZZ $-$ baseline with 95\% intervals, circled
    where the Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$ within the baseline family. All four panels
    share one $N$ axis so the two sampling regimes can be read against each
    other; the rare-enriched pool exhausts at $N=2000$ (shaded).
    Under the natural class prior (rare classes $0.83\%$) the ordering reverses
    between $N=2000$ and $N=5000$ (band in~c), and QSVM-ZZ leads XGBoost by
    $+0.015$ at $N=10^4$ (Holm $p=0.008$). Drawing the training sets from the
    rare-enriched pool used by the submitted version (rare $10\%$, a
    $12\times$ enrichment) removes the reversal entirely (d).
    The crossover is not an artefact of re-tuning: freezing the hyper-parameters
    at their fixed values reproduces it at the same location
    (Table~\ref{tab:crossover-arms}).
    SVM-RBF is retained throughout because it, not XGBoost, is the strongest
    classical baseline at $N=10^4$ ($0.774$ vs $0.771$); QSVM-ZZ never beats it
    significantly at any $N$ on this dataset.}
  \label{fig:learning-curve}
\end{figure*}

  \caption{%
    \textbf{Regime map: 110 controlled comparisons of QSVM-ZZ against six
    baselines.}
    Each cell is one paired comparison over 10 runs, Holm-corrected within its
    baseline family (\emph{entanglement}: QSVM-Z; \emph{classical kernel}:
    linear, poly2, RBF; \emph{strong tabular}: random forest, XGBoost).
    Verdicts are encoded by both colour and glyph so the figure survives
    greyscale printing.
    The distribution is the result: 21 QSVM-favourable, 21 classical-favourable
    and 68 inconclusive cells. We report this rather than a headline win because
    the honest summary of the evidence is that the quantum kernel helps in
    identifiable regimes---low label prior, shifted attack composition, larger
    training sets---and does not help, or loses, elsewhere. A single aggregate
    number over this table would hide exactly the structure the paper is about.}

\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig11_unsw_transfer.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{What transfers to UNSW-NB15, and what does not.}
    (a) Mean macro-$F_1$ over 10 runs on the official UNSW-NB15 test set;
    (b) paired $\Delta F_1$ against each baseline, circled where the
    Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$.
    The dimension-selection rule transfers (Fig.~\ref{fig:c1-selection}) and so
    does the entanglement ablation, which is roughly twice as large here
    ($+0.045$, Holm $p=0.002$ at $N=10^4$) as the largest significant effect we
    measure on NSL-KDD ($+0.022$ at $N=5000$), and which holds at every
    $N\ge 2000$ rather than at a single point. The sample-complexity crossover
    does \emph{not} transfer: against both tree ensembles the difference keeps its sign at
    every $N$ we tested. Against the kernel baselines it does change sign, and
    QSVM-ZZ leads SVM-RBF from $N\ge 2000$ ($+0.040$, Holm $p=0.006$ at
    $N=10^4$)---the opposite of NSL-KDD, where that comparison is never
    significant. The rare-class advantage does not transfer either, because the
    UNSW rare classes are simply easier (recall $0.95$--$0.99$ for all
    seven models).
    A single dataset would have supported a broader claim than the evidence
    warrants.}
  \label{fig:unsw-transfer}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig1_zzfeaturemap_circuit.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{The ZZFeatureMap at the operating point of this study:
    $n=4$ qubits, $r=2$ repetitions, full entanglement.}
    (a) Layered structure with the entangling block abstracted; each layer
    applies a Hadamard, a single-qubit phase $P(2x_i)$, and then
    $U_{\mathrm{ent}}(\mathbf{x})$.
    (b) Gate-level decomposition of that block: every qubit pair contributes a
    $\mathrm{CNOT}$--$R_Z(\varphi_{ij})$--$\mathrm{CNOT}$ trio, so full
    entanglement on four qubits gives $\binom{4}{2}=6$ trios per layer and
    $24$ two-qubit gates in total at $r=2$.
    The pairwise phase $\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=2(\pi-x_i)(\pi-x_j)$ is the
    only place where feature interactions enter the kernel; removing this block
    yields the ZFeatureMap control used throughout the ablation. The quadratic
    growth of these terms with $n$ is what drives the concentration reported in
    Fig.~\ref{fig:width}.}
  \label{fig:circuit}
\end{figure*}

    \textbf{Each contribution answers one question with its own protocol.}
    Rows map a contribution to the data it consumes, the protocol it runs, the
    metrics it computes, and where it is reported. The four protocols are
    deliberately not interchangeable: C1 fixes the representation before any
    downstream metric is inspected, C2 varies only the entangling layer, C3

    Holding everything else fixed in each row is what allows a difference to be
    attributed to the factor named in that row.}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig3_pipeline.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{End-to-end pipeline. The three-stage rule replaces the Pareto search
    of the submitted version.}
    Raw records are one-hot encoded to $122$ dimensions, reduced by an ANOVA
    $F$-test to $K=20$ features, projected to $n^\ast$ principal components, and
    scaled into $[0,\pi]$ before the quantum feature map; classification is an
    SVC on the precomputed Gram matrix. The block below fixes $n^\ast$ and is
    the component that changed: the weighted Pareto objective has been removed
    in favour of a lexicographic rule with no tunable weights
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:erratum} explains why). The branch above is the
    entanglement-free control that differs from the main path in exactly one
    component. Every transformer is fit on the training fold only; in the
    primary arm the whole front-end is refit at each $N$, so no representation
    ever sees more data than the classifier it feeds.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

  \caption{%
    \textbf{Entanglement moves the kernel geometry far more than it moves the
    score.}
    (a) Kernel--target alignment for the two feature maps, paired within each of
    ten runs; every run is a line, and the markers are means with $95\%$
    confidence intervals. (b) The paired differences.
    Removing the entangling layer costs $0.1378$ alignment
    $[0.1267,\,0.1489]$, an interval far from zero, but only $0.0114$
    macro-$F_1$ $[-0.0054,\,0.0281]$, an interval that contains it. Alignment is
    therefore a sensitive probe of what the entangler does to the kernel, and a
    poor predictor of what it does to accuracy --- which is why we report both
    and rest no claim on alignment alone.}
  \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_revision/fig12_width_concentration.pdf}
  \caption{%
    \textbf{Widening the circuit destroys the quantum kernel, and the kernel
    geometry says why.}
    (a) The full sample-complexity sweep re-run at $K=80$, where the selection
    rule returns $n^\ast=8$. QSVM-ZZ falls below every baseline at every
    training-set size; all $32$ paired comparisons are classical-favourable
    after Holm correction, and the entanglement ablation reverses sign, with the
    ZZ map now below its entanglement-free control.
    (b) Dispersion of the off-diagonal Gram entries against circuit width, with
    the fitted decay exponent of $\mathrm{std}(n)\propto n^{-\alpha}$. The ZZ
    map concentrates roughly twice as fast as the Z map at $K=20$
    ($\alpha=0.59$ vs.\ $0.29$), consistent with its $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise
    phase terms growing quadratically where the Z map's grow linearly.
    (c) Macro-$F_1$ against that dispersion across $n=4$ to $10$ at fixed
    $K=20$: the ZZ kernel tracks it ($r=+0.77$) while the Z kernel, which barely
    concentrates, does not ($r=+0.32$).
    Taken together these panels say that the four-qubit circuit is not a
    convenience but a constraint: the regime in which this kernel helps is
    bounded above by concentration, and the bound is measurable from the Gram
    matrix alone.}
  \label{fig:width}
\end{figure*}
