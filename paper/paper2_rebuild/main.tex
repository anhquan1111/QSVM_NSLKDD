\begin{abstract}
% TODO(tac gia): got lai con 150-200 tu truoc khi nop.
An intrusion detector is deployed on the strength of its probabilities, yet
quantum-kernel classifiers are almost always evaluated on accuracy alone. We
benchmark a \QSVM{} against Random Forest, XGBoost, an MLP and an SVM-RBF
baseline under one zero-leakage pipeline and one recalibration protocol, on
NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15. We first show that the natural way to pose the
question is unavailable: the rare-attack union consists entirely of attack
records, so on that subset every bin has accuracy one by construction and the
expected calibration error collapses to one minus the mean predicted
probability --- an identity we verify to
$\pidentityMaxDev$. It measures confidence on known attacks, not
calibration. Measured where both classes are present, the picture is
consistent across both corpora and both circuit widths: over $\pnRuns$
paired runs with Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests the quantum kernel is better
calibrated than both tree ensembles in every setting
($\Delta\ECE=\pdNslRf$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdNslRfHolm$ on NSL-KDD;
$\pdUnswSixRf$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdUnswSixRfHolm$ on UNSW-NB15), while an
MLP is better calibrated than the quantum kernel everywhere. Giving the
trees the full one-hot representation improves their ranking and worsens
their calibration, so the comparison is made in the trees' most favourable
calibration setting. Post-hoc recalibration --- Platt, isotonic or
temperature --- helps the quantum kernel and no other model, and lowering
the decision threshold recovers the rare attack classes for the
margin-based models and not for the tree ensembles. One property of the
score distributions accounts for both: a forest emits a distinct
probability for $\psatRfDistinct\%$ of test records against
$\psatQsvmDistinct\%$ for the quantum kernel, and a monotone recalibrator
cannot separate records that already share a score. Of $\pcenTotal$
Holm-corrected comparisons, $\pcenQsvm$ favour the quantum kernel,
$\pcenBase$ favour a classical baseline and $\pcenIncon$ are inconclusive:
the advantage is confined to calibration, and it is absent from ranking.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}

intrusion detection, NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% TODO(tac gia): doc lai va nới rong neu can. Khung lap luan da day du.
An intrusion detection system is rarely deployed as a bare classifier. It is
deployed behind a threshold, feeding a queue that a human or a downstream
policy has to triage, and the quantity that governs that queue is a
probability rather than a label. A detector that is right often but confident
at the wrong moments is harder to operate than one that is slightly less
accurate and honest about it. Accuracy therefore answers only half of the
deployment question.

Quantum-kernel classifiers are almost always evaluated on the other half
alone. Since quantum feature maps were proposed as a route to expressive
kernels~\cite{havlicek2019,schuld2019hilbert}, and since separations were
established for carefully constructed learning problems~\cite{liu2021speedup},
the intrusion-detection literature has measured them by accuracy and $F_1$.
On tabular data of this kind, gradient-boosted trees and random forests
usually win that contest, which makes an accuracy-only comparison both
unfavourable to the quantum kernel and uninformative about deployment.

This paper asks the other half of the question: when one of these models
reports a probability, how far can that number be trusted? We find first
that the natural way to pose the question --- restrict the estimate to the
rare attack classes that matter most --- is unavailable for a structural
reason, and that the quantity such a restriction produces is not a
calibration error at all. We then measure calibration where it is
well-defined, on two corpora and at two circuit widths.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A measurement result: on a subset drawn by attack category, every
        record carries the same label, so binned calibration error is
        identically one minus mean confidence and cannot detect
        miscalibration. We verify the identity to $\pidentityMaxDev$ across
        every model and run, and give the non-degenerate counterpart.
  \item A calibration benchmark of a quantum kernel against four baselines
        under one pipeline and one recalibration protocol, on two corpora and
        at two circuit widths, over $\pnRuns$ paired runs with
        Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests.
  \item The finding that the quantum kernel is better calibrated than both
        tree ensembles in every setting tested, and is not better than an
        MLP.
  \item The finding that post-hoc recalibration helps the quantum kernel and
        degrades every other model, robust to the choice of recalibrator.
  \item A mechanism for that finding, measured rather than conjectured: the
        tree ensembles' scores are quantised and saturated, a monotone
        recalibrator cannot separate tied records, and the same property
        explains why no decision threshold recovers the rare classes for
        them.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Relation to the submitted version}
This paper is a substantially revised version of an earlier submission, and
five of that version's claims do not survive the revised protocol. We state
them here rather than in a footnote.

(i) \emph{The headline rare-attack result is withdrawn.} That version
reported $\ECE_{\text{rare}}=\psubQsvmEceRare$ for the quantum kernel
against $\psubRbfEceRare$ for the RBF kernel and concluded it was the most
trustworthy model on the rare classes. The estimate was computed on
$\psubNRareTest$ rare test records, and
Section~\ref{sec:identity} shows that on a subset of one class the quantity
is not a calibration error at all. Both the measurement and its
interpretation are replaced.

(ii) \emph{The low-data claim is narrowed.} That version placed the quantum
kernel first from $N\geq200$ onward. Measured where calibration is
well-defined it leads at $N=\pldNmin$ only, and the MLP leads at every
larger budget (Table~\ref{tab:lowdata}).

(iii) \emph{The balanced-operating-point claim is withdrawn.} The
best-calibrated model under a balanced prior is \ppsBalWinner{}, not the
quantum kernel (Table~\ref{tab:priorshift}).

(iv) \emph{The recalibration claim is corrected in both directions.} That
version concluded Platt scaling suits margin-based quantum \emph{and} SVM
models. It helps the quantum kernel alone; the classical SVM degrades
slightly ($\pRbfPlattDelta$). The finding is narrower than claimed and, once
isotonic and temperature scaling are added, more robust.

(v) \emph{The identical-footing claim was false.} That version reused cached
quantum models whose training subset differed from the subset used to fit
their calibrator, while the tree baselines were fitted and calibrated on the
same data. Every model here is trained from scratch on one subset.

None of the five was raised by a reviewer; the submission was declined
without review. They were found by re-running the protocol at a scale the
original design could not reach.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Quantum Kernel and the \ZZ{}-FeatureMap}
\begin{definition}[Quantum kernel]
For a data-dependent unitary $U:\mathbb{R}^n\!\to\!\mathcal{U}(2^n)$ with
state map $\phi(\mathbf{x})=U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, the
induced quantum kernel is
$K^{\mathrm{Q}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')=
|\langle 0|U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}')U(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle|^2$.
\end{definition}
We use the depth-$r$ \ZZ{}-FeatureMap with full entanglement,
$U^{(r)}_{\ZZ}(\mathbf{x})$, whose entangling layer encodes pairwise
feature products as quantum phases. The four-qubit, $r{=}2$
configuration is fixed by the companion study's hardware-aware Pareto
analysis~\cite{companion} and is reused here without modification, so the
present paper isolates classifier reliability rather than embedding
choice.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration}
\begin{definition}[Calibration]
A probabilistic classifier producing $\hat{p}(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]$ is
\emph{perfectly calibrated} if
$\Pr\!\big(y{=}1\mid\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}){=}q\big)=q$ for all $q$.
\end{definition}
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and the Brier score are the standard
scalar summaries of the gap from perfect
calibration~\cite{naeini2015ece,brier1950}, and a reliability diagram is
the graphical counterpart. Tree ensembles are often over-confident on the
minority class~\cite{guo2017calibration}, motivating post-hoc
recalibration such as Platt scaling~\cite{platt1999svm}, a logistic map
from decision scores to probabilities. Calibration is rarely reported in
quantum-IDS studies, and never compared against strong tabular learners.

The gap is not an oversight so much as an inheritance. The applied
quantum-IDS literature grew out of demonstrations that a quantum feature map
can separate classes at all, and the natural way to report such a
demonstration is accuracy against a classical baseline. Payares and
Mart\'{i}nez-Santos~\cite{payares2021dos} compare quantum and classical
models on denial-of-service detection by accuracy; Suryotrisongko and
Musashi~\cite{suryotrisongko2022dga} evaluate a hybrid
quantum--classical architecture on botnet traffic on the same footing;
Kalinin and Krundyshev~\cite{kalinin2023qids} survey quantum techniques for
intrusion detection without reporting a probability-quality measure. None
of the three reports a calibration curve, a Brier score, or an ECE, and
none compares against a gradient-boosted tree. Table~\ref{tab:coverage}
places them against the three gaps this paper addresses.

That matters more here than it would elsewhere, because the accuracy
comparison is the one a four-qubit kernel is least likely to win. On tabular
data the tree ensembles are the reference
point~\cite{breiman2001rf,chen2016xgboost}, and a representation compressed
to four components to fit a NISQ budget gives the quantum branch little room.
Measuring only accuracy therefore both understates what a quantum kernel
offers and leaves the deployment question --- whether the reported
probability can be acted on --- entirely open.

The calibration literature supplies the other half of the setting.
Guo~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{guo2017calibration} established that modern
high-capacity classifiers are systematically over-confident and that the
effect worsens with capacity, which motivated the post-hoc correction we
apply uniformly here. Two features of our results sit awkwardly with that
picture and are worth flagging early: on this corpus the models are
\emph{under}-confident rather than over-confident, and the correction helps
exactly one of the five. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} argues that both
follow from a property of the score distributions rather than from model
capacity.

\begin{proposition}[Platt scaling preserves ranking]
\label{prop:platt}
Platt scaling applies a strictly increasing logistic transform
$\sigma(As+B)$, $A{>}0$, to a score $s$. Because the transform is
monotone, the induced ordering of samples is unchanged; hence
threshold-free ranking metrics (AUC-ROC, AUC-PR) are invariant to Platt
scaling, while binning-based calibration metrics (ECE) and the Brier
score are not.
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:platt} is the methodological reason our comparison
can apply one uniform Platt protocol to every model without distorting
the ranking comparison: any AUC difference reflects the raw model
ordering, while ECE/Brier differences reflect calibration.

\subsection{Strong Tabular Baselines}
Random Forests~\cite{breiman2001rf} and gradient-boosted trees
(XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}) are the strongest non-deep models on
tabular data and the comparators a reviewer expects for any practical IDS
claim; we adopt both alongside SVM-RBF~\cite{cortes1995svm}.

\section{Why the rare-attack subset cannot answer the question}
\label{sec:identity}

The obvious way to ask whether a detector is trustworthy on the attacks that
matter is to restrict the calibration estimate to those attacks. On both
corpora this does not work, for a reason that is structural rather than
statistical.

The rare union is defined by attack category --- User-to-Root and
Remote-to-Local on NSL-KDD, and Worms, Shellcode, Backdoor and Analysis on
UNSW-NB15. Every record in it is an attack, so its binary label is one.
Under equal-frequency binning each bin $B$ then contains only positives and
$\mathrm{acc}(B)=1$ identically, so
\begin{equation}
\ECE = \sum_B \frac{|B|}{n}\,\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B)-\mathrm{conf}(B)\bigr|
     = \sum_B \frac{|B|}{n}\,\bigl(1-\mathrm{conf}(B)\bigr)
     = 1-\bar{p},
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
where $\bar p$ is the mean predicted attack probability on the subset. The
right-hand side does not depend on the binning at all. The quantity is a
measure of confidence on known attacks, close in spirit to recall; it is not
a calibration error, and no model can be penalised by it for being
over-confident.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig1_identity.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Measured calibration error on the rare subset against
  $1-\bar p$, for every model and run on both corpora: they are the same
  number. (b) Calibration is only measurable where per-bin accuracy varies,
  which on the rare subset it cannot.}
  \label{fig:identity}
\end{figure}

We verify Eq.~\eqref{eq:identity} numerically for every model and every run
(Fig.~\ref{fig:identity}(a)): the largest absolute deviation is
$\pidentityMaxDev$. We also verify the
converse, that the estimate on the full test split is not degenerate: there
both classes are present, per-bin accuracy varies, and the identity fails by
a wide margin. All calibration numbers below are therefore measured on the
full test split of each corpus, and we report the rare-subset quantity under
its own name where it is informative.

\section{Reliability Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{What we measure}
For a test set $\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and calibrated
probabilities $\hat p_i$, partitioned into $B$ equal-frequency bins,
\begin{equation}
\ECE = \sum_{b}\frac{|B_b|}{n}\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B_b)-\mathrm{conf}(B_b)\bigr|,
\qquad
\Brier = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat p_i-y_i)^2 ,
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{acc}$ and $\mathrm{conf}$ the empirical accuracy and mean
confidence in a bin. Equal-frequency rather than equal-width binning keeps
every bin populated on a skewed score distribution. We report
AUC-PR alongside, because ranking quality and probability quality are
separate axes and a model can lead on one while trailing on the other.

\subsection{One recalibration protocol for every model}
Each model is reduced to a one-dimensional score before recalibration: the
decision function for the kernel machines, the log-odds of the
positive-class probability for the tree ensembles and the MLP. A single
logistic map is then fitted on the \emph{training} scores and applied to the
test scores, so no test label enters the calibrator. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:platt} this leaves AUC-PR and AUC-ROC untouched, which
is what allows one protocol to be applied uniformly without distorting the
ranking comparison.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Uniform probability and reliability protocol}
\label{alg:protocol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{input} train split $(X_{\mathrm{tr}},y_{\mathrm{tr}})$, test
       split $(X_{\mathrm{te}},y_{\mathrm{te}})$, model $M$, bins $B$
\STATE fit $M$ on $(X_{\mathrm{tr}},y_{\mathrm{tr}})$
\STATE $s_{\mathrm{tr}} \leftarrow \mathrm{score}(M,X_{\mathrm{tr}})$,\;
       $s_{\mathrm{te}} \leftarrow \mathrm{score}(M,X_{\mathrm{te}})$
       \COMMENT{decision function, or log-odds for probabilistic models}
\STATE fit recalibrator $g$ on $(s_{\mathrm{tr}},y_{\mathrm{tr}})$
       \COMMENT{no test label is seen}
\STATE $\hat p \leftarrow g(s_{\mathrm{te}})$
\STATE partition $\hat p$ into $B$ equal-frequency bins
\STATE \textbf{return} $\ECE$, $\Brier$, AUC-PR from
       Eq.~(\ref{eq:metrics})
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol} states the procedure applied identically to
every model. Two properties matter. The recalibrator sees only training
scores and training labels, so no test information reaches the probability;
and reducing each model to a one-dimensional score before recalibration is
what makes a single protocol applicable to a kernel machine, a tree ensemble
and a neural network at once. Where the literature usually compares
calibration numbers produced by whatever each library happens to emit, every
number here comes from the same seven lines.

\subsection{The five questions}
We ask the same question under five conditions, and the conditions are
chosen because they correspond to things that change in deployment rather
than to things that are convenient to vary.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Operating calibration.} Calibration on the full test split
        of each corpus (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
  \item \textbf{Representation.} Whether the tree ensembles are
        disadvantaged by sharing the quantum kernel's low-dimensional input
        (Fig.~\ref{fig:refarm}).
  \item \textbf{Label scarcity.} Calibration against training-set size
        (Table~\ref{tab:lowdata}).
  \item \textbf{Class-prior shift.} Calibration when the deployment mix
        differs from the benchmark mix (Table~\ref{tab:priorshift}).
  \item \textbf{Temporal drift.} Calibration on the harder KDDTest-21
        split, whose records the original benchmark's classical learners
        all misclassified.
\end{enumerate}

Each is reported with paired tests and effect sizes rather than with point
estimates, and we report the conditions in which the quantum kernel does not
lead as prominently as those in which it does.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

% TODO(tac gia): pipeline chi tiet, sieu tham so. Giu mo ta cua ban cu.

All models share a zero-leakage front end: one-hot encoding,
\textsc{SelectKBest}, PCA and a min--max map onto $[0,\pi]$, every
transformer fitted on the training subset alone. Reliability is evaluated
over $\pnRuns$ independent stratified training subsets of $\pnTrain$
records. Every model, quantum and classical, is trained from scratch on the
same subset; the submitted version reused cached quantum models whose
training subset differed from the one used to fit their calibrator, so the
quantum and tree branches were not in fact on an identical footing.

\subsection{Corpora}
NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd} is the de-duplicated revision of KDD'99
and remains the reference corpus for this line of work. Its training split
holds $\pcorpNslTrain$ records at $\pcorpNslAttackTrain\%$ attack, its test
split $\pcorpNslTest$ at $\pcorpNslAttackTest\%$, and attacks are grouped
into $\pcorpNslCats$ categories of which User-to-Root and Remote-to-Local
are the rare and security-critical ones. The test split deliberately
contains attack types absent from training, which is what makes it a test of
generalisation rather than of memorisation --- and, as
Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}(a) shows, what makes every model on it
under-confident.

UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015unsw} was built to address the age of KDD'99: it
is synthesised from modern traffic with a contemporary attack mix. Its
training split holds $\pcorpUnswTrain$ records at
$\pcorpUnswAttackTrain\%$ attack and its test split $\pcorpUnswTest$ at
$\pcorpUnswAttackTest\%$, over $\pcorpUnswCats$ attack categories. The rare
union here is Worms, Shellcode, Backdoor and Analysis. The two corpora
differ in era, in attack taxonomy and in the direction of their prior shift
between splits, which is what makes agreement across them informative.

\subsection{Test splits}
NSL-KDD is evaluated on the full KDDTest+ split, $\pnTest$ records of which
$\pnRare$ belong to the rare union; UNSW-NB15 on its full test split,
$\punswTest$ records of which $\punswRare$ are rare. The submitted version
used a $\pnTestOld$-record sample of KDDTest+ holding $\pnRareOld$ rare
records. The reason for the small sample was the cost of the quantum Gram
matrix; evaluating the feature map in closed form removes that cost, so the
constraint no longer binds.

\subsection{Circuit width}
The companion study's selection rule returns $\plegacyQubits$ qubits on
NSL-KDD and $\punswQubits$ on UNSW-NB15, so comparing the corpora at their
selected widths would change the dataset and the width together. We
therefore run UNSW-NB15 at both widths and report both.

\subsection{What the quantum branch costs}
A recommendation is only actionable if the recommended model is affordable,
and for a quantum kernel that is not a detail: the cost of the Gram matrix
is precisely why the submitted version evaluated on
$\pnTestOld$ records rather than $\pnTest$. Evaluating the feature map in
closed form removes it. Averaged over five runs at $N=\pnTrain$ training
records and $\pcostNtest$ test records, the quantum kernel fits in
$\pcostQsvmFit$\,s and scores the full test split in $\pcostQsvmPred$\,s.
The classical RBF kernel, which carries the same support-vector arithmetic
at inference, takes $\pcostRbfPred$\,s on the same split --- within a few
percent. Fitting is the cheapest of the five models, because the Gram
matrix reduces to one dense product; only the MLP, at $\pcostMlpFit$\,s, is
an order of magnitude slower to train.

The cost objection to quantum kernels is therefore not operative at this
scale. It returns at larger $N$, where the Gram grows quadratically, and on
real hardware, where each entry needs repeated circuit execution rather
than one matrix product. Both are outside what we measure.

\subsection{Statistics}
We report paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with bootstrap confidence
intervals and $d_z$, Holm-corrected within baseline families. Ten runs are
the minimum for which this is meaningful: with five paired runs the smallest
attainable two-sided $p$ is $0.0625$, so no comparison can reach
significance whatever the effect size.

\subsection{The census}
Before the individual comparisons, the tally. Across every setting, metric
and baseline, the protocol produces $\pcenTotal$ Holm-corrected
comparisons: $\pcenQsvm$ favour the quantum kernel, $\pcenBase$ favour a
classical baseline, and $\pcenIncon$ are inconclusive. There is no general
advantage here, and a paper that reported only its wins could have drawn a
very different picture from the same runs.

The structure inside the tally is the result. Split the comparisons by what
they measure and they separate almost cleanly. On the calibration metrics
the quantum kernel takes $\pcenCalQsvm$ of $\pcenCalTotal$; on the ranking
metrics it takes $\pcenRankQsvm$ of $\pcenRankTotal$. It does not rank
better than any baseline anywhere, and it calibrates better than half of
them everywhere. That is the shape of the claim this paper makes, and the
reason a single scalar comparison would have hidden it.

\subsection{Calibration against the tree ensembles}
The quantum kernel is better calibrated than both tree ensembles in every
setting tested. On NSL-KDD the paired difference is $\pdNslRf$
$[\pdNslRfLo,\pdNslRfHi]$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdNslRfHolm$, $d_z=\pdNslRfDz$)
against Random Forest and $\pdNslXgb$ $[\pdNslXgbLo,\pdNslXgbHi]$
($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdNslXgbHolm$) against XGBoost. On UNSW-NB15 at the
selected width the same comparisons give $\pdUnswSixRf$
($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdUnswSixRfHolm$, $d_z=\pdUnswSixRfDz$) and
$\pdUnswSixXgb$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdUnswSixXgbHolm$), and at
$\plegacyQubits$ qubits $\pdUnswFourRf$ and $\pdUnswFourXgb$. Eight of eight
comparisons are corrected-significant in the quantum kernel's favour, and
the effect is far larger on UNSW-NB15 than on NSL-KDD.

\subsection{What the calibration curves look like}
Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}(a) plots the curve that Eq.~\eqref{eq:metrics}
summarises. The shape is the same for all five models and it is not the one
the calibration literature leads us to expect. Tree ensembles are usually
reported as over-confident~\cite{guo2017calibration}; here every model is
\emph{under}-confident, lying above the diagonal in $\pcurveQsvmUnder$ of
$\pcurveNbins$ bins for the quantum kernel and similarly for the rest. The
observed attack fraction exceeds the predicted probability across most of
the range.

The reason is the corpus rather than the models. KDDTest+ contains attack
types absent from the training split, so records that a model scores low on
familiarity grounds are nonetheless attacks. A detector tuned on this
benchmark will under-alert if its probabilities are taken at face value,
and that applies to the quantum kernel as much as to the baselines.

\input{tables/percat_table}

\subsection{Which attacks the probabilities can be trusted on}
Section~\ref{sec:identity} showed that a calibration estimate restricted to
one attack category is exactly one minus the mean confidence on it. That
quantity is still worth reporting --- under its own name, and per category
rather than pooled. Table~\ref{tab:percat} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}(b) give it.

The ordering differs sharply by category. On Probe the quantum kernel is the
weakest of the five ($\pcatProbeQsvm$ against $\pcatProbeXgb$ for XGBoost),
and on Normal it is neither the most nor the least conservative. On the two
rare categories the ordering reverses: the quantum kernel assigns the
highest mean attack probability to \catUtoRName{} ($\pcatUtoRQsvm$ against
$\pcatUtoRRf$ for Random Forest), and on R2L it is second behind the RBF
kernel. This is the defensible form of the rare-attack claim: not that the
quantum kernel is better calibrated there --- that statement is not
measurable --- but that it is the least reluctant of the five to call a
rare attack an attack.

One observation applies to every model and matters more than the ordering.
The mean probability on both rare categories falls below the decision
threshold for all five: at a default cut-off, none of these detectors would
raise an alert on a typical R2L or U2R record. The families differ in how
far below they sit, not in whether they clear it.

\subsection{Where the quantum kernel does not lead}
The MLP is better calibrated than the quantum kernel in every setting
($\pdNslMlp$ on NSL-KDD, $p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdNslMlpHolm$; $\pdUnswSixMlp$ on
UNSW-NB15, $p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdUnswSixMlpHolm$), and we report this as
prominently as the wins. Against the classical RBF kernel the outcome is
dataset-dependent: on NSL-KDD the RBF kernel is ahead ($\pdNslRbf$,
$p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdNslRbfHolm$), while on UNSW-NB15 the comparison is
inconclusive at both widths ($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdUnswSixRbfHolm$ at
$\punswQubits$ qubits). The claim this paper supports is a claim about tree
ensembles, not a claim that a quantum kernel is the most reliable model
available.

\subsection{Are the tree baselines handicapped?}
The trees share the quantum kernel's low-dimensional representation, which
invites the objection that they are compared in a form that suits them
badly. We repeat them on the $\prefKTwentyDims$ selected features and on the
full $\prefFullDims$ one-hot encoding, holding every hyper-parameter fixed
so that only the representation changes.

More features do not make the trees better calibrated. Random Forest's
calibration error moves from $\prefPcaRfEceFull$ to $\prefFullRfEceFull$ and
XGBoost's from $\prefPcaXgbEceFull$ to $\prefFullXgbEceFull$ --- slightly
worse in both cases, and in neither case enough to approach the quantum
kernel. Ranking moves the other way and by a larger margin: Random Forest's
AUC-PR improves from $\prefPcaRfAucPr$ to $\prefFullRfAucPr$. The point is
not that the richer representation harms the trees much; it is that it does
not help their calibration at all, so the comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:main} is made in the trees' most favourable calibration
setting rather than a cramped one.

\subsection{When post-hoc recalibration helps}
Platt scaling reduces the quantum kernel's calibration error from
$\pQsvmPlattBefore$ to $\pQsvmPlattAfter$ ($\pQsvmPlattDelta$) and is the
only model of the five it helps: Random Forest moves by $\pRfPlattDelta$ and
XGBoost by $\pXgbPlattDelta$, both worse, and the classical SVM degrades
slightly ($\pRbfPlattDelta$).

This is not an artefact of choosing a logistic map. Isotonic regression and
temperature scaling give the same picture: on the quantum kernel all three
land within a narrow band ($\pcalQsvmPlatt$, $\pcalQsvmIso$,
$\pcalQsvmTemp$ against $\pcalQsvmNone$ uncalibrated), while on both tree
ensembles all three are worse than leaving the native probability alone
(Random Forest $\pcalRfNone$ uncalibrated against $\pcalRfIso$ isotonic).

A global improvement can hide a local regression, so we repeat the
comparison on the rare categories alone. It does not: the quantum kernel's
mean confidence there is $\pcalrareQsvmPlatt$ under Platt,
$\pcalrareQsvmIso$ under isotonic and $\pcalrareQsvmTemp$ under temperature
scaling, the highest of the five under every recalibrator, and no
recalibrator pushes the tree ensembles above $\pcalrareRfTemp$. Temperature
scaling is the only combination that lifts any model over the decision
threshold on the rare union, and it does so for the RBF kernel
($\pcalrareRbfTemp$) rather than for the quantum kernel.

\subsection{Training-set size}
Reliability is not uniform in the amount of training data.
Table~\ref{tab:lowdata} sweeps $N\in\{\pldNmin,\ldots,\pldNmax\}$, refitting
every model on each subset. The best-calibrated model at the smallest size
is \pldWinnerMin{} ($\pldQsvmMin$ against $\pldMlpMin$ for the MLP and
$\pldRfMin$ for Random Forest) --- the one condition in this study where the
quantum kernel leads the field outright. Its relative standing falls as $N$
grows: at $N=\pldNmax$ it is $\pldQsvmMax$ against $\pldMlpMax$ for the MLP.
The advantage, such as it is, belongs to the low-data regime, which is also
the regime in which a four-qubit kernel is cheapest to run.

\subsection{Class-prior shift}
A benchmark's class mix is not the deployment mix, and a calibrator fitted
under one can fail under the other. We rebuild three operating conditions
from the full test split rather than from a fixed sample
(Table~\ref{tab:priorshift}): a balanced mix ($\ppsBalN$ records), an
attack-heavy mix ($\ppsAttN$), and a DoS-only mix ($\ppsDosN$). The
best-calibrated model is \ppsBalWinner{} on the balanced mix ($\ppsBalMlp$
against $\ppsBalQsvm$ for the quantum kernel), \ppsAttWinner{} on the
attack-heavy mix, and \ppsDosWinner{} on the DoS-only mix ($\ppsDosQsvm$).
The quantum kernel is ahead of both tree ensembles under all three
conditions ($\ppsBalRf$, $\ppsAttRf$, $\ppsDosRf$ for Random Forest), which
is the same ordering as Table~\ref{tab:main} and not a separate effect.

\subsection{Temporal drift}
KDDTest-21 retains the records of KDDTest+ that the original benchmark's
classical learners all misclassified, and is the standard stand-in for
drift on this corpus. The ordering is unchanged there: the quantum kernel is
better calibrated than Random Forest by $\pdDriftRf$
$[\pdDriftRfLo,\pdDriftRfHi]$ ($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdDriftRfHolm$,
$d_z=\pdDriftRfDz$) and than XGBoost by $\pdDriftXgb$
($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdDriftXgbHolm$), behind the MLP by $\pdDriftMlp$
($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdDriftMlpHolm$), and inconclusive against the RBF kernel
($p_{\text{Holm}}=\pdDriftRbfHolm$). Counting KDDTest-21 alongside the four
settings of Table~\ref{tab:main}, the quantum kernel beats both tree
ensembles in ten of ten corrected comparisons.

\subsection{Ranking is not reliability, as a decomposition}
So far the separation of the two axes has been an observation about two
columns of a table. The Murphy decomposition turns it into an identity.
Writing $\Brier=\mathrm{REL}-\mathrm{RES}+\mathrm{UNC}$ over the same bins
Eq.~\eqref{eq:metrics} uses, the reliability term $\mathrm{REL}$ is the
squared calibration gap, the resolution term $\mathrm{RES}$ measures how far
a model's bins depart from the base rate --- that is, how much it
discriminates --- and $\mathrm{UNC}$ depends only on the data and is
therefore identical across models.

Table~\ref{tab:murphy} and Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}(b) give the result, and
it is the cleanest statement of this paper's thesis. The best resolution
belongs to \pbrBestRes{} ($\pbrRfRes$), and so does the \emph{worst}
reliability ($\pbrRfRel$). The best reliability belongs to \pbrBestRel{}
($\pbrMlpRel$) on a resolution that is lower ($\pbrMlpRes$). The quantum
kernel's resolution, $\pbrQsvmRes$, is within a thousandth of the MLP's,
while its reliability is $\pbrQsvmRel$: it discriminates as well and
calibrates less well. A single Brier number would have hidden all of this,
because the two terms move in opposite directions and partly cancel.

\subsection{Choosing an operating point}
Section~\ref{sec:results} left an open question: if the mean probability on
the rare categories is below the threshold for every model, what threshold
would be needed, and what does it cost? Table~\ref{tab:threshold} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}(a) answer it, and the answer separates the model
families more sharply than any calibration number does.

For the margin-based models, lowering the threshold works. The quantum
kernel's U2R recall rises from $\pthQsvmRecallHalf$ at the default to
$\pthQsvmRecallStar$ at its macro-$F_1$ optimum $\tau^{\ast}=\pthQsvmStar$,
at a false-positive rate on normal traffic of $\pthQsvmFpr$. The MLP reaches
$\pthMlpRecallStar$ and the RBF kernel $\pthRbfRecallStar$ on the same
sweep.

For the tree ensembles it does not. Random Forest moves only from
$\pthRfRecallHalf$ to $\pthRfRecallStar$, and XGBoost from
$\pthXgbRecallHalf$ to $\pthXgbRecallStar$; across the entire sweep neither
exceeds $\pthRfRecallBest$ and $\pthXgbRecallBest$ respectively. Their
probability mass on U2R is concentrated so near zero that no threshold
recovers the class. This is a deployment consequence of the calibration
result rather than a restatement of it: a practitioner who responds to
missed rare attacks by lowering the threshold will be rewarded with a
margin-based detector and will not be with a tree ensemble.

\section{Why the Families Differ}\label{sec:mechanism}

Three results so far point the same way: recalibration helps the quantum
kernel and not the tree ensembles; lowering the threshold recovers the rare
class for the margin-based models and not for the trees; and the trees hold
the best resolution together with the worst reliability. A single property
of the score distributions accounts for all three, and it is measurable
rather than conjectural.

A post-hoc recalibrator --- Platt, isotonic or temperature --- is a
\emph{monotone} map. It can move scores along the line but it cannot
separate two records that received the same score. Whatever resolution the
raw scores lack, no such map can supply. So the question is how much
distinct information the raw scores carry before recalibration begins.

They differ by more than an order of magnitude. Over $\pnRuns$ runs on
KDDTest+, the quantum kernel emits a distinct probability for
$\psatQsvmDistinct\%$ of test records and the RBF kernel for
$\psatRbfDistinct\%$, while Random Forest emits one for $\psatRfDistinct\%$
and XGBoost for $\psatXgbDistinct\%$. A forest's output is a vote fraction
over a finite ensemble, and on a training set of this size most leaves are
pure, so the fraction collapses onto a handful of values at the extremes:
$\psatRfRaw\%$ of Random Forest's raw probabilities fall below $0.01$ or
above $0.99$, against $\psatQsvmRaw\%$ for the quantum kernel, whose
decision function is continuous and never saturates.

This explains the three results together. Ties and saturation give the trees
high resolution, because the extremes separate the classes sharply --- and
poor reliability, because the extremes are more confident than the data
warrant. Recalibration cannot repair it, since the map is monotone and the
tied records stay tied; indeed it makes matters worse, pushing
$\psatRfCal\%$ of Random Forest's calibrated probabilities into the
saturated tails. And no threshold recovers the rare classes for a model
whose mass on them is already concentrated at zero.

The corollary is not about quantum computing. Any classifier whose scores
are continuous and unsaturated will recalibrate well on this task, and any
classifier whose scores are quantised and saturated will not. The quantum
kernel is on the favourable side of that divide because it is a margin
machine, not because it is quantum. What the quantum kernel adds over the
classical margin machines on this evidence is not calibration quality but
the resolution it achieves at that quality --- $\pbrQsvmRes$ against
$\pbrRbfRes$ for the RBF kernel, at four qubits.

\subsection{Where the account is incomplete}
Saturation explains the contrast between the kernel machines and the tree
ensembles. It does not explain the model that actually wins. The MLP
saturates $\psatMlpRaw\%$ of its raw outputs --- much closer to the trees
than to the kernels --- and emits distinct values for $\psatMlpDistinct\%$
of records, again intermediate. On the saturation account it should
calibrate somewhere between the two families. It does not: it has the
lowest reliability term of the five, $\pbrMlpRel$, against $\pbrQsvmRel$ for
the quantum kernel and $\pbrRfRel$ for Random Forest.

So saturation is necessary for the failure mode and not sufficient for the
success. A plausible reading is that what matters is not saturation alone
but whether the saturated mass is placed correctly: a network trained by
cross-entropy is optimising a proper scoring rule directly and can be
confident \emph{and} right, whereas a forest's vote fraction saturates as a
by-product of leaf purity and carries no such guarantee. We cannot settle
that from the evidence here --- it would need an ablation over training
objectives, which is a different experiment --- and we flag it rather than
present the mechanism as more complete than it is.

That the strongest baseline in this study is a small multilayer perceptron
rather than a tree ensemble is itself worth stating plainly, because the
quantum-IDS literature rarely includes one. On the deployment question this
paper asks, the model to beat is not the one the accuracy benchmarks
suggest.

\section{Reliability Regime Map}\label{sec:regime}

\input{tables/regime_table}

Table~\ref{tab:regime} collects every condition in one place: which model is
best calibrated, and whether the quantum kernel is better calibrated than
both tree ensembles. Read down the last column, the answer is uniform ---
the quantum kernel beats both tree ensembles in every condition tested,
across two corpora, two circuit widths, a drift split, four training
budgets and three deployment priors. Read down the middle column, the
answer is almost as uniform in the other direction: the MLP is the
best-calibrated model nearly everywhere.

The two conditions where the quantum kernel leads outright are the smallest
training budget and the DoS-only prior. Both are narrow, and we do not
claim they generalise beyond what is in the table. But they are not
arbitrary: the low-data regime is where a four-qubit kernel is cheapest to
evaluate, and it is the regime in which the classical baselines have least
to work with.

\paragraph{A decision rule}
For a practitioner the map reduces to three statements. If the deployment
mix and training budget resemble the benchmark, a small neural network is
the better-calibrated choice and a quantum kernel buys nothing. If the
candidate set is restricted to kernel machines and tree ensembles --- which
is the usual comparison in this literature --- the quantum kernel is the
better-calibrated choice, consistently and by a margin that survives
correction. And in either case the probabilities on the rare categories
should not be used at a default threshold without the recalibration of
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Construct validity}
Binned calibration error is an estimator with a free parameter, and a
different bin count gives a different number. We use equal-frequency bins
throughout and report the Brier score, which needs no binning, alongside
every ECE; the two agree on the ordering in Table~\ref{tab:main}. The
identity of Section~\ref{sec:identity} is a stronger warning about the same
family of estimators: a scalar summary can be well-defined arithmetically
and still measure nothing, and the only defence is to check what varies in
the subset one has restricted to.

\subsection{Internal validity}
The recalibrators are fitted on the training split. For a model that can
memorise that split --- the tree ensembles above all --- the fitted map is
in-sample, which flatters it. This biases the comparison \emph{towards} the
trees, and they lose anyway, so the conclusion is safe in direction; but the
magnitudes in Table~\ref{tab:recal} would change with a held-out
calibration split, and the finding that recalibration helps the quantum
kernel alone should be read with that caveat. A second exposure is the
hyper-parameters, which are inherited from the companion study rather than
re-tuned here; re-tuning per model could move any row of
Table~\ref{tab:main}, and we did not do it because tuning each family on its
own criterion would reintroduce exactly the asymmetry that
Section~\ref{sec:setup} removes.

\subsection{External validity}
Two corpora, both offline benchmark captures with known idiosyncrasies, and
both with attack types in the test split that are absent from training ---
which is why every model is under-confident in
Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}(a). Whether the ordering holds on live traffic is
not established here. The quantum kernel is evaluated by exact statevector
simulation, so shot noise and device noise are absent; the companion study
finds finite-shot estimation tracks the statevector kernel closely on
accuracy, but whether it perturbs \emph{calibration} is open and is the
obvious next experiment.

\subsection{Statistical conclusion validity}
Ten paired runs is the minimum at which a two-sided Wilcoxon test can reach
$\alpha=0.05$, so the design has no power to spare. Comparisons we report as
inconclusive --- the quantum kernel against the RBF kernel on three of four
settings --- may be real effects this design cannot resolve, and should not
be read as evidence of equivalence. The comparisons we do claim clear Holm
correction within their baseline family, but the families were fixed in
advance rather than chosen after seeing the results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

% TODO(tac gia): doc lai truoc khi nop.
We set out to ask whether a quantum-kernel intrusion detector can be trusted
at the level of its probabilities, and found first that the question has to
be posed carefully. Restricting a binned calibration estimate to the rare
attack classes yields a quantity that is identically one minus mean
confidence, because every record in that subset carries the same label; it
measures confidence on known attacks and cannot detect over-confidence at
all. Reported as a calibration error it would overstate what has been
measured.

Measured where both classes are present, the result is narrow but it holds
everywhere we looked. Across two corpora and two circuit widths, over
$\pnRuns$ paired runs with Holm-corrected tests, the quantum kernel is
better calibrated than both tree ensembles in all eight comparisons, and the
margin is several times larger on UNSW-NB15 than on NSL-KDD. It is not
better calibrated than a small multilayer perceptron anywhere, and against a
classical RBF kernel the outcome depends on the corpus. Giving the trees the
full one-hot representation improves their ranking without improving their
calibration, so the comparison is made on terms that favour them. Post-hoc
recalibration helps the quantum kernel and no other model, whether the
recalibrator is Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling.

Underneath the benchmark there is a mechanism, and it is not a quantum one.
A post-hoc recalibrator is a monotone map and cannot separate records that
share a score. The tree ensembles emit a distinct probability for only
$\psatRfDistinct\%$ of test records against $\psatQsvmDistinct\%$ for the
quantum kernel, and saturate $\psatRfRaw\%$ of their outputs at the extremes
against $\psatQsvmRaw\%$. That single property accounts for all three of
our secondary findings at once: why the trees hold the best resolution and
the worst reliability, why no recalibrator repairs them, and why lowering
the threshold recovers the rare classes for the margin-based models alone.
The quantum kernel lands on the favourable side of that divide because it
is a margin machine, and what it adds over the classical margin machines is
resolution at a comparable calibration, not calibration itself.

The practical reading is modest. A four-qubit kernel is not the most
reliable model available for this task, but among the families an
intrusion-detection practitioner would actually reach for first --- the tree
ensembles --- it is the better-calibrated choice, and it is the one that
post-hoc recalibration can still improve. Two things would sharpen the
picture: a held-out calibration split, which would remove the in-sample
advantage the tree ensembles currently enjoy, and a finite-shot evaluation,
which would establish whether any of this survives the noise of a real
device. Neither is expensive, and the second is the question our simulation
is constitutionally unable to answer.
