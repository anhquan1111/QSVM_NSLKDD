\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared a four-qubit \ZZ{} quantum kernel with an entanglement-free control and five
classical learners for binary intrusion detection, varying one factor at a time under a paired
protocol, with every model tuned by cross-validation, on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15. Under the natural class prior, $11$ of
the $42$ comparisons of the training-set-size sweep favour the quantum kernel, $9$ favour a
classical baseline and $22$ are inconclusive. Over all $109$ comparisons the counts are $20$,
$21$ and $68$, and $20$, $18$ and $71$ under a Benjamini--Hochberg correction over the whole
map.

The map contains three findings that can be tested on other data. First, on NSL-KDD and on the
shared four-component embedding, the difference between the quantum kernel and XGBoost changes
sign between $N=2000$ and $N=5000$ and is significant at $N=10^4$; at $N=5000$ it is significant
only on the pre-set grid for $C$. It remains after duplicated test records are removed. On
UNSW-NB15 the sign does not change; the gap to XGBoost only narrows, from $-0.090$ at $N=100$ to $-0.020$
at $N=10^4$. Against SVM-RBF no training-set size gives a significant difference on the pre-set
grid with the default RBF width, and against XGBoost given all $122$ features
none was detected at $N\ge5000$. Second, the width rule,
with thresholds set on NSL-KDD, returns $n^{\ast}=6$ on UNSW-NB15 in $10/10$ subsets at
$\varepsilon\le0.05$ and in $7/10$ at $\varepsilon=0.1$, although on large subsets an
eight-qubit circuit scores higher there. Third, on NSL-KDD in noiseless simulation an
eight-qubit circuit is behind every baseline, including the \Zmap{} control, in all $48$ comparisons, and across seven widths lower off-diagonal
dispersion of the \ZZ{} Gram matrix goes with lower macro-$F_1$ ($r=+0.77$). The quantum kernel
also degrades faster than every baseline under feature perturbation.

These results come from classical simulation of the circuits, with a single noise model
checked at a single operating point, and from training sets of at most $10^4$ records at four
qubits. The median absolute difference among inconclusive comparisons is $0.014$ macro-$F_1$, and ten
runs per comparison resolve differences of that size only in part.

Three questions remain open. Executing the same kernels on quantum hardware would show whether
the noise check holds outside simulation. Repeating the width analysis with projected quantum
kernels \cite{ref19}, whose values are computed from reduced states, would show whether the
association between dispersion and accuracy is specific to fidelity kernels. Why additional
training data helps the quantum kernel more than XGBoost on NSL-KDD but not on UNSW-NB15 is
not answered by this study. The code, protocol files and per-run results are released so that
each of these can start from the same protocol.
