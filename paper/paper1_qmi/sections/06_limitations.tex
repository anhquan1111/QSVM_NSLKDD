\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Simulation, not hardware.} Every kernel in this study was computed by classical
simulation, and no circuit was executed on a quantum processor. The main results use exact
statevector fidelities, which carry neither sampling error nor gate error. The noise check of
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-noise} adds both for one configuration: the four-qubit operating point, one
backend-derived noise model (\texttt{FakeManilaV2}), $512$ shots per kernel entry, $200$
training and $300$ test records. Its result applies to that configuration only. Effects outside
the noise model, such as drift of the device calibration between characterisation and
execution, are not represented, and the check says nothing about other backends, deeper
circuits or larger training sets. The cost model of Sec.~\ref{sec:featuremap} counts logical
two-qubit gates. Routing onto the simulated backend raises the four-qubit circuit from $24$ to
$44$ CNOT gates, and this overhead depends on the coupling map of the device. The width
results of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-width} were obtained without noise, so they show that the decline with width occurs without gate error. They do not show
what hardware with lower error rates, another feature map or a rescaled bandwidth would do,
since we tested none of these.

\emph{Statistical power.} Each comparison rests on ten paired runs. The smallest two-sided
Wilcoxon $p$-value that ten runs can produce is $0.00195$, above the Holm threshold of
$0.05/109$ for the whole map, which is why that correction returns no verdict at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:regime-mult}). The median absolute difference among inconclusive cells is
$0.014$ macro-$F_1$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:regime}), and ten runs resolve only part of the differences
of that size. We did not run equivalence tests, so no inconclusive cell should be read as
evidence that two models perform equally. Cells near the threshold can change verdict when the data change
slightly: removing duplicated test records moved four of $108$ cells across it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:res-dedup}).

\emph{Training-set size.} The quantum kernel was trained on at most $10^4$ records at four
qubits and $2\times10^4$ at eight, and never on a full training partition ($125{,}973$ records
for NSL-KDD). The Gram matrix grows with the square of $N$, which set this limit. The
reversal against the tree ensembles is observed at $N=5000$ and $N=10^4$; we did not test
whether it holds at larger $N$. The classical reference trained on the whole partition
(XGBoost, macro-$F_1$ $0.804$) is a single point, not a paired comparison. The
enriched pool stops at $N=2000$ because the ten runs would otherwise share most of their rare
records, so the question whether enrichment removes the reversal remains open.

\emph{Hyper-parameter grids.} The grid for $C$ was fixed before the experiments, and at
$N\ge5000$ the selected value often lies on its edge. Widening it at $N=5000$ and $N=10^4$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:res-n}) left the verdicts at $N=10^4$ in place but made the lead over XGBoost at
$N=5000$ inconclusive, and the wider grid is again reached at its edges in some runs. The other
cells of the map were not re-tuned on the wider grid. The width $\gamma$ of the RBF kernel was
not tuned at all, so the RBF baseline may be weaker than it could be.

\emph{Data.} Both datasets are older public benchmarks, and the task is binary throughout. R2L
and U2R are evaluated as part of the attack class; the rare-class scores of
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-rare} measure how often those records are detected as attacks, not whether
they are told apart from other attack types. A multi-class formulation was not tested, and the
rare-class analysis exists only for NSL-KDD. Our duplicate check removes test records whose raw
features exactly equal those of a training record. Near-duplicates, and repeated records within
the training partition, were not removed. In $10$ of the $260$ NSL-KDD trainings of that
check the original training subset could not be rebuilt exactly, and both sides of the
comparison use the rebuilt subset. Absolute scores are measured on the official test
partitions and are not comparable to studies that report results on random splits of the
training data.

\emph{Design choices held fixed.} The study uses one feature map, the \ZZ{} map with two
repetitions and full entanglement, and its entanglement-free control. The feature budget $K=20$
comes from a plateau rule on a linear proxy and a subset rich in rare attacks; it is not the
accuracy optimum of the proxy, and on the full training partition the same rule would give
$K=30$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-embed}). The thresholds of the selection rule were
set on NSL-KDD and applied unchanged to UNSW-NB15, where its choice of $n^{\ast}=6$ holds in
$10/10$ subsets at $\varepsilon\le0.05$ and in $7/10$ at $\varepsilon=0.1$. Other feature maps,
other repetition counts or other thresholds could move the boundaries of the regime map. The
link between Gram-matrix dispersion and macro-$F_1$ was measured at seven widths and a single
training-set size, and we report it only as an association.

\emph{Baselines.} The classical baselines are linear, polynomial and RBF support vector
machines, a random forest and XGBoost, all tuned by cross-validation on the same data as the quantum kernel;
the selection rule differs between the kernel machines and the ensembles
(Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), and the RBF width is not tuned.
We did not include CatBoost or deep tabular models such as TabNet or FT-Transformer. A fair comparison with the latter
would need its own tuning-budget protocol, and adding it would turn a controlled kernel
benchmark into a survey of tabular methods. The regime map therefore locates the quantum kernel
relative to these five learners, not relative to the strongest tabular method available.
