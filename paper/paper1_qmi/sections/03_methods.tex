\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Feature map, kernel and cost}\label{sec:featuremap}

The \ZZ{} feature map on $n$ qubits with $r$ repetitions \cite{havlicek2019} prepares
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:featuremap}
  |\phi(\bm{x})\rangle = \bigl[\,U_{\Phi}(\bm{x})\,H^{\otimes n}\,\bigr]^{r}\,|0\rangle^{\otimes n},
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the Hadamard gate and $U_{\Phi}$ is diagonal in the computational basis,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:phase}
  U_{\Phi}(\bm{x}) = \exp\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i Z_i
    + \mathrm{i}\!\!\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\!\!\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi_{ij}(\bm{x})\,Z_iZ_j\Bigr),
  \qquad \varphi_{ij}(\bm{x}) = 2(\pi-x_i)(\pi-x_j).
\end{equation}
The kernel is the squared fidelity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel}
  K^{\mathrm{Q}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}) = \bigl|\langle\phi(\bm{x}^{\prime})|\phi(\bm{x})\rangle\bigr|^{2},
\end{equation}
used in a soft-margin support vector machine on the precomputed Gram matrix
\cite{cortes1995,scholkopf2002,schuld2019}. We use $r=2$ and pairwise terms between all qubit
pairs. Removing the pairwise term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase} and keeping everything else gives the
\Zmap{} feature map, the control in every entanglement comparison; we call the two models
\QSVM{} and \QSVM{}-\Zmap{}.

With all pairs entangled, each repetition places $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise terms, and each term
compiles to two CNOT gates, so the circuit has $2r\binom{n}{2}$ two-qubit gates: $24$ at $n=4$
and $112$ at $n=8$. We charge a circuit the gate count
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost}
  Q(n) = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{raw}}(n)}{Q_{\mathrm{raw}}(10)}, \qquad
  Q_{\mathrm{raw}}(n) = nr + 5\cdot 2r\tbinom{n}{2} = 10n^{2}-8n \quad (r=2),
\end{equation}
which weights a two-qubit gate five times a single-qubit rotation and is normalised over the
candidate widths $n\le10$. The selection rule below uses only the fact that $Q$ increases with
$n$, so any increasing cost gives the same width. The $\Theta(N^2)$ kernel evaluations needed
for training are not part of $Q$, since they do not depend on $n$.

Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives a second-order expansion of $K^{\mathrm{Q}}$ around
$\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}$ for $r=1$. Its leading terms are the squared coordinate differences
and the squared differences of the pairwise phases. Each phase is a linear combination of
monomials of degree at most two, the features of a degree-2 polynomial kernel, which is why
SVM-poly2 is among the baselines. At $r=2$ the
expansion no longer holds in this form, so we use it only to choose that baseline.

\subsection{Pipeline and embedding-dimension rule}\label{sec:pipeline}

A record passes through one-hot encoding of the categorical fields, an ANOVA $F$-test that
keeps $K$ features, principal component analysis to $n$ components, and a min--max scaler to
$[0,\pi]$, the domain of the phases in Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase}. The quantum kernels act on the
scaled vector $\bm{z}\in[0,\pi]^n$. The classical baselines are a linear, a degree-2
polynomial and an RBF support vector machine, a random forest and XGBoost; they receive the same
$n$ principal components, standardised instead of scaled to $[0,\pi]$. The comparison therefore
ranks learners on one fixed embedding. We report XGBoost and a random forest on all one-hot
features separately, as a reference outside the paired protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-n}).

Every transformer is fitted on the training records of the run and size at hand and then
applied to the test records. In the training-set-size sweeps, feature selection, projection and
scaling are refitted at every $N$, so no representation uses more data than the classifier it
feeds.

The width $n$ is chosen before any classifier is trained, by a lexicographic rule with no
weights. Let $\mathcal{N}=\{2,\dots,10\}$, let $V(n)$ be the cumulative explained variance of
the first $n$ components, and let $\mathrm{KTA}(n)$ be the kernel--target alignment
\cite{cristianini2002} of the \ZZ{} Gram matrix $G$ with the binary labels
$\bm{y}\in\{-1,+1\}^m$,
$\mathrm{KTA}=\langle G,\bm{y}\bm{y}^{\top}\rangle_F/(\|G\|_F\,\|\bm{y}\bm{y}^{\top}\|_F)$,
computed on a stratified subset of $m=300$ training records.

\begin{definition}[Three-stage selection]
\label{def:three-stage}
With thresholds $T=0.85$ and $\varepsilon=0.05$ fixed in advance,
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{N}_1 &= \{\, n \in \mathcal{N} : V(n) \ge T \,\}, \\
  \mathcal{N}_2 &= \bigl\{\, n \in \mathcal{N}_1 : \mathrm{KTA}(n) \ge
                   (1-\varepsilon)\max_{m\in\mathcal{N}_1}\mathrm{KTA}(m) \,\bigr\}, \\
  n^{\ast} &= \arg\min_{n \in \mathcal{N}_2} Q(n).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

Stage~1 keeps widths whose components retain most of the variance, stage~2 keeps those whose
kernel is close to the best alignment among them, and stage~3 takes the cheapest remaining
circuit. Higher alignment at comparable training error is associated with a tighter
generalisation bound \cite{cortes2012}; we use alignment only as a screen and derive no bound
from it. Wide fidelity kernels also concentrate as the number of qubits grows
\cite{thanasilp2024}, which is a second reason to prefer the narrowest admissible circuit. With
$K=20$ the rule returns $n^{\ast}=4$ on NSL-KDD (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-embed}). On UNSW-NB15 we apply
it unchanged with $K=35$, where it returns $n^{\ast}=6$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-unsw}).

\subsection{Data and protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

\emph{Data.} NSL-KDD \cite{ref4} is used with its official partitions: $125{,}973$ training and
$22{,}544$ test records, with $41$ features that one-hot encoding expands to $122$. The training
partition is $53.5\%$ normal traffic, $36.5\%$ DoS, $9.3\%$ Probe, $0.8\%$ R2L and $0.04\%$ U2R.
UNSW-NB15 \cite{ref25} is used with its official training ($175{,}341$) and test ($82{,}332$)
sets. The task on both is binary, normal traffic against attack. The main metric is
macro-$F_1$, the mean of the $F_1$ scores of the two classes. For the rare classes (R2L and U2R
on NSL-KDD) we report $F_1$ on the test records of those classes. All of them are attacks, so
this score equals $2R/(1+R)$, where $R$ is their recall.

On the official split absolute scores are lower than the values near $0.99$ often quoted for
NSL-KDD. Trained on all $122$ features and the whole training partition, XGBoost reaches
macro-$F_1$ $0.804$ on KDDTest$^+$ and a random forest $0.777$; on a random $80/20$ split
inside KDDTrain$^+$ the same models reach $0.999$ and $0.998$. We use the official partitions
throughout, and $0.804$ is the reference for every absolute score in this paper.

A test record is a duplicate when its raw features (the $41$ NSL-KDD features or the $34$
UNSW-NB15 features, labels excluded) equal those of any training record. This holds for $664$
of $22{,}544$ NSL-KDD test records ($2.9\%$) and $20{,}851$ of $82{,}332$ UNSW-NB15 test records
($25.3\%$). Sec.~\ref{sec:res-dedup} repeats the size comparisons without them.

\emph{Subsets and runs.} Each result is computed over ten runs with seeds $100$ to $109$.
Training subsets are drawn stratified on four labels (normal, DoS, Probe, and R2L and U2R
together), so that every subset contains at least one rare record. Within a run the subsets are
nested, $\mathcal{D}_{100}\subset\mathcal{D}_{200}\subset\cdots$, so differences across $N$
come from added data and not from a new sample. Two training pools are used
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). The \emph{natural} pool keeps the class prior of the training
partition and is used for the training-set-size sweeps up to $N=10^4$ (NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15) and
$2\times10^4$ (eight-qubit sweep). The \emph{enriched} pool raises R2L and U2R to $10\%$ of each
subset. It is used for the experiments at $N=1000$ and for a control sweep that stops at
$N=2000$: beyond that, the ten runs would share too many rare records ($59\%$ at $N=5000$) to
count as independent.

\emph{Test sets.} The training-set-size sweeps are scored on the full official test set. The
entanglement comparison, the width sweep and the noise check use one stratified sample of $300$
KDDTest$^+$ records. The shift experiments use $1000$-record test mixtures drawn from KDDTest$^+$
or, for the temporal shift, from KDDTest-21, at the stated proportions of attack traffic or
attack type.

\emph{Hyper-parameters.} Every model, quantum and classical, is tuned by the same procedure:
five-fold stratified cross-validation on macro-$F_1$ over $C\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1,3,5,10\}$ for the
support vector machines, and over a grid of tree count, depth, leaf size, learning rate and
subsampling for the ensembles. For the support vector machines we take the simplest setting
within one standard error of the best mean score; for the ensembles, the best mean. Because the
Gram matrix does not depend on $C$, the quantum kernel is computed once per subset and only the
classifier is refitted. \QSVM{}-\Zmap{} uses the $C$ selected for \QSVM{}, so the two differ
only in the feature map. Results are reported for two arms. In the \emph{tuned} arm the search
runs on the training subset itself, at every $N$ and in every run. In the \emph{frozen} arm it
runs once on a separate tuning set of $2000$ records, disjoint from all training and test
subsets, and the selected values ($C=3$ for both quantum kernels) are kept at every $N$.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

Every comparison is paired within run. For run $i$ we form
$\Delta_i = m(\text{\QSVM{}}) - m(\text{baseline})$ on the same training subset and the same
test records, and report the mean of $\Delta$ with a $t$-based $95\%$ confidence interval, a
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the paired effect size $d_z$ \cite{ref32}. Pairing
removes the subset-to-subset variance that otherwise dominates comparisons of learners on
resampled data \cite{ref31}.

The $p$-values are Holm-adjusted within baseline families: the \Zmap{} control alone, the three
classical kernels, and the two tree ensembles. A comparison favours one side when the adjusted
$p<0.05$ and the interval excludes zero; otherwise it is inconclusive. The \Zmap{} family has one
member, so its $p$-values are unadjusted. The $110$ comparisons of the regime map fall into $56$
families, so under a global null at most about $2.8$ families would be expected to contain a
false verdict.
Sec.~\ref{sec:regime-mult} therefore also counts the verdicts under Benjamini--Hochberg and
Holm over the whole map. With ten runs the smallest attainable two-sided Wilcoxon $p$ is
$2/2^{10}=0.00195$. Correlations are reported with Fisher-$z$ intervals, and two correlations
are compared with a $z$ test on their Fisher transforms.

\subsection{Implementation and reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

Experiments use NumPy 2.4, SciPy 1.17, scikit-learn 1.8 \cite{ref30}, XGBoost 3.3, Qiskit 2.3
\cite{ref29}, qiskit-machine-learning 0.9 and Qiskit Aer 0.17. Kernels are exact statevector
fidelities. The state of each record is computed once and the Gram matrix is a product of
state matrices. It agrees with the Qiskit fidelity kernel to within $5\times10^{-15}$, and each
execution of a sweep stops unless the two agree to $10^{-9}$ on $150$ random inputs. XGBoost runs on one
thread with the histogram method, since with several threads its result depends on the thread
count.

Code, protocol files, per-run results and figure scripts are released at
\url{https://github.com/haodpsut/nisq-qsvm-nids-benchmark} (release tag to be added on
submission). Four scripts check the reported numbers against the per-run records:
\texttt{audit\_c4.py} recomputes $100$ statistics with an implementation independent of the
pipeline, \texttt{audit\_figures.py} re-derives the $36$ plotted quantities,
\texttt{verify\_lemma1.py} checks the expansion of Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} against the exact
kernel ($15$ checks) and \texttt{verify\_noise10.py} re-derives the noise check of
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-noise} ($40$ checks).
