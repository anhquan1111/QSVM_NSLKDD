\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:protocol} lists the training pool, training-set size and test set behind each
result below. Unless stated otherwise, a comparison is a paired difference \QSVM{} minus
baseline over ten runs, reported as a mean with a $95\%$ confidence interval and a
Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon $p$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:stats}).

\subsection{Embedding size}\label{sec:res-embed}

\input{figs/fig_ksweep}
\input{figs/fig_c1_selection}

The number of retained features $K$ and the number of qubits $n$ are coupled through the
selection rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline}. Fig.~\ref{fig:ksweep}(a) sweeps $K$ with a linear SVM
proxy. Without projection, macro-$F_1$ rises steadily to $0.9727$ at $K=122$. After projection to
four components it peaks at $0.9283$ at $K=80$, and at $K=20$ it is $0.9007$. These proxy scores
are cross-validated inside KDDTrain$^+$ and are not comparable with test-set scores.

Applied at $K=20$, the variance gate $V(n)\geq0.85$ admits $n\in\{4,\dots,10\}$. The maximum
alignment is $\mathrm{KTA}_{\max}=0.2439$ at $n=5$, so the alignment gate (threshold $0.2317$)
keeps $\{4,5,6\}$, and the cost stage selects $n^{\ast}=4$, a circuit with $24$ two-qubit gates
(Fig.~\ref{fig:c1-selection}). Here the alignment gate does not change the outcome: $n=4$ is
also the narrowest width that passes the variance gate. Across budgets the same rule returns $n^{\ast}=4,7,8,9$ for
$K=20,40,80,122$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ksweep}b). With more features retained, the same variance is
spread over more components, $V(n)$ falls at every $n$, and the variance gate admits only wider
circuits. Moving from $K=20$ to $K=80$ raises the proxy's macro-$F_1$ by $0.028$ and the
two-qubit gate count from $24$ to $112$. The budget itself depends on the subset used to choose
it: the curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:ksweep}(a) is computed on all of KDDTrain$^+$ under the natural
prior, and on it the $0.01$ plateau rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline} would give $K=30$ rather than
$K=20$. We keep $K=20$, which was fixed before the experiments; Sec.~\ref{sec:res-width} reports
the quantum kernel at $K=80$.

\subsection{Entanglement ablation}\label{sec:res-ent}

\input{figs/fig_ablation}
\input{figs/fig_perrun}

At $n^{\ast}=4$ we compare \QSVM{} with the same circuit without its entangling layer
(\QSVMZ{}) and with five classical baselines. Entanglement raises kernel--target
alignment by $\Delta\mathrm{KTA}=+0.1378$ $[+0.1267,+0.1489]$ ($d_z=8.91$). The macro-$F_1$
difference is $+0.0114$ $[-0.0054,+0.0281]$ ($p=0.23$, unadjusted in a one-member family); the interval contains
zero, and no significant difference was detected (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}).

The mean macro-$F_1$ of the seven models (Fig.~\ref{fig:perrun}) is XGBoost $0.8503$
$[0.8412,0.8595]$, \QSVM{} $0.8469$ $[0.8321,0.8616]$, random forest $0.8446$, SVM-RBF
$0.8362$, \QSVMZ{} $0.8355$, SVM-poly2 $0.8327$ and SVM-linear $0.8137$. The intervals of
the first two overlap, so we read this as an ordering of point estimates.

\subsection{Distribution shift}\label{sec:res-shift}

\input{figs/fig_priorshift}

We apply four shifts to the $N=1000$ models (Fig.~\ref{fig:priorshift}). In the taxonomy of
\cite{ref33}, the first two change the class prior and the mix of attack types, the third
moves to harder test records and the fourth perturbs the features.

\emph{Class prior.} When the attack fraction of the test mixture is raised to $30$, $50$ and
$70\%$, \QSVM{} separates from its \Zmap{} control ($+0.0237$ at $50\%$ and $+0.0367$ at $70\%$,
both significant after correction), and from the linear kernel only at $30\%$ ($+0.0303$; the
comparison is inconclusive at $50$ and $70\%$). It degrades faster than both tree ensembles: over the shift it loses $0.032$ macro-$F_1$, against $0.021$ for XGBoost and $0.025$
for the random forest. At $70\%$ attacks the difference against XGBoost favours the classical
model ($-0.0242$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.039$). The larger loss is a difference of point estimates:
paired over runs, the loss of \QSVM{} does not differ significantly from that of XGBoost or of
the random forest (Wilcoxon $p=0.11$ and $0.43$, unadjusted).

\emph{Attack composition.} On a $50/50$ mixture of normal and DoS traffic, \QSVM{} is ahead of
all four kernel baselines, with effect sizes from $1.56$ to $3.02$. No significant difference
was detected against either tree ensemble ($+0.0014$ and $+0.0006$).

\emph{Harder test records.} KDDTest-21 is the part of KDDTest$^+$ left after removing the
records that all $21$ reference classifiers of \cite{ref4} label correctly; it comes from the
same collection and differs in difficulty, not in time. On mixtures drawn from it, \QSVM{} loses more than its \Zmap{} control
and more than the linear and polynomial kernels; all three comparisons favour the baseline. The
comparisons against SVM-RBF and both ensembles are inconclusive.

\emph{Feature perturbation.} We add Gaussian noise with standard deviation
$\sigma\in\{0.01,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ to the input features, which are scaled to $[0,1]$ before the
pipeline, and clip the result to $[0,1]$; every model receives the same perturbed records. The
macro-$F_1$ of \QSVM{} falls faster than that of every baseline. All six comparisons of
degradation slope favour the baseline, with slope differences from $-0.69$ to $-1.11$ macro-$F_1$ per unit $\sigma$ and
effect sizes above $2.8$ in magnitude.

\subsection{Training-set size}\label{sec:res-n}

\input{figs/fig_learning_curve}
\input{tables/ref_arm}

We vary $N$ from $100$ to $10^4$ under the natural class prior and score every model on the full
KDDTest$^+$ set (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning-curve}a,~c). All seven models use the same
four-component input, so the comparison ranks learners on one embedding.

At $N=100$ the ranking is XGBoost $0.7802$, random forest $0.7695$, \QSVMZ{} $0.7613$,
SVM-RBF $0.7296$, SVM-linear $0.7261$, \QSVM{} $0.6989$, SVM-poly2 $0.6890$. The quantum kernel is next to last and
its difference from XGBoost, $-0.0812$, is the largest in the sweep. At $N=10^4$ the order is
\QSVM{} $0.7855$, \QSVMZ{} $0.7787$, SVM-RBF $0.7740$, random forest $0.7728$, XGBoost
$0.7706$, SVM-linear $0.7335$, SVM-poly2 $0.7114$. The difference against XGBoost changes sign between $N=2000$ and $N=5000$
($-0.0129$ to $+0.0100$) and is significant at $N=5000$ on the pre-set grid of $C$
($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.027$, $d_z=1.22$)
and at $N=10^4$ ($+0.0149$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0078$, $d_z=1.07$).
The classical models contribute to this change. \QSVM{} rises at every step, while XGBoost
peaks at $0.8007$ at $N=1000$ and falls to $0.7706$, the random forest falls from $0.7913$ to
$0.7728$ over the same range, and SVM-RBF from $0.7919$ at $N=2000$ to $0.7740$. The test set is
fixed, so the intervals reflect variation of the training subset only.

\input{tables/novel_attacks}

KDDTest$^+$ contains $3{,}750$ attack records of $17$ types that never occur in KDDTrain$^+$,
besides $9{,}083$ attack records of types seen in training. Table~\ref{tab:novel} splits the
attack recall along this line. On seen types, recall rises or holds from $N=1000$ to $N=10^4$
for every model except SVM-poly2. On novel types it falls for every model: from $0.550$ to
$0.376$ for XGBoost, from $0.518$ to $0.356$ for the random forest, from $0.595$ at $N=2000$ to
$0.447$ for SVM-RBF, and from $0.423$ to $0.335$ for the \Zmap{} control, against $0.458$ to
$0.426$ for \QSVM{}. Specificity on normal traffic moves by at most $0.05$. The fall of the
classical scores is therefore concentrated in the novel attack types. At $N=10^4$, \QSVM{} is
ahead of XGBoost on both groups (seen $+0.021$ $[+0.008,+0.035]$, novel $+0.050$
$[+0.022,+0.078]$, ten paired runs). Against SVM-RBF it is ahead on seen types ($+0.018$
$[+0.005,+0.031]$) and on normal traffic ($+0.022$ $[+0.015,+0.028]$) but not on novel types
($-0.021$ $[-0.087,+0.045]$). We did not test why the classical models lose novel types as the
training set grows.

\emph{Fixed hyper-parameters.} Table~\ref{tab:crossover-arms} repeats the sweep with
hyper-parameters held at the values selected once. All six baseline--arm combinations change sign
between $N=2000$ and $N=5000$, the same step as in the tuned arm, so the reversal does not depend
on tuning at each $N$.

\emph{Baselines.} The reversal differs by baseline. Against SVM-RBF, on the pre-set grid with the
default $\gamma$, the difference turns positive, but no cell of the sweep reaches significance
after correction. The two checks below change this at large $N$: \QSVM{} is significantly ahead
of SVM-RBF on a wider grid for $C$ at $N=10^4$, and with $\gamma$ tuned under the
one-standard-error rule at $N=5000$ and $N=10^4$. On the pre-set grid, significance against
XGBoost is reached for $N\geq5000$, against the random forest only at $N=10^4$, and against the
linear and polynomial kernels from $N=1000$ and $N=2000$ respectively. Both checks start from
the retrained models of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-dedup} rather than from the main runs. At $N\geq5000$
their mean scores differ from those of the main runs by at most $0.002$ (\QSVM{} $0.7836$
against $0.7820$ at $N=5000$, SVM-RBF $0.7721$ against $0.7740$ at $N=10^4$), and below
$N=5000$ by at most $0.001$.

% (runners/run_cgrid_extension.py, runners/analyze_cgrid_extension.py).
\emph{Regularisation grid.} At $N=10^4$ the value of $C$ selected for \QSVM{} and for SVM-RBF
is the largest on the grid ($C=10$) in $7$ of $10$ runs, and SVM-linear takes the smallest
($C=0.1$) in every run at $N\geq5000$. We therefore repeated the tuning of the four support vector machines at
$N=5000$ and $N=10^4$ over $C\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,0.5,1,3,5,10,30,100\}$, on the same training
subsets and folds, with the tree ensembles unchanged. Refitting with the original values
reproduced the scores of the retrained models to within $1.34\times10^{-4}$. On the wider grid the mean score of
\QSVM{} changes by at most $0.004$ ($0.7836$ to $0.7832$ at $N=5000$, $0.7854$ to $0.7890$ at
$N=10^4$), while SVM-RBF loses $0.014$ and $0.009$: cross-validation selects larger values of
$C$ that score lower on the test set. At $N=10^4$ every verdict holds, and \QSVM{} is also
significantly ahead of SVM-RBF ($+0.0256$ $[+0.0140,+0.0372]$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0059$). At
$N=5000$ the comparison with XGBoost becomes inconclusive ($+0.0099$ $[-0.0003,+0.0201]$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.098$), and so does the comparison with the \Zmap{} control. The wider grid
is itself reached at its edges in some runs (\QSVM{} at $C=100$ in $5$ of $10$ runs at
$N=10^4$, SVM-RBF in $7$ of $10$ at $N=5000$, SVM-linear at $C=0.03$ in $18$ of $20$). We
keep the pre-set grid for all other results; the lead over XGBoost at $N=5000$ depends on it,
the lead at $N=10^4$ does not.

% So: results/nslkdd/c4_rbf_gamma/rbf_gamma_summary.csv, rbf_gamma_pairwise.csv
% (runners/run_rbf_gamma.py, runners/analyze_rbf_gamma.py).
\emph{Width of the RBF kernel.} Everywhere else $\gamma$ is left at its default. We tuned it
jointly with $C$ at every $N$, over $\gamma=m/d$ with $m\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,10,30\}$, where
$m=1$ is the default, under the same one-standard-error rule (smallest $\gamma$ first, then
smallest $C$). Refitting with the default reproduced the scores of the retrained models exactly. Tuning
$\gamma$ helps SVM-RBF only on small subsets. At $N=200$ its score rises from $0.7566$ to
$0.7836$, and it is then significantly ahead of \QSVM{} ($-0.0553$ $[-0.0922,-0.0185]$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.018$). From $N=2000$ cross-validation selects narrower kernels (median
$m=10$ at $N=2000$ and $m=30$, the edge of the grid, at $N=5000$ and $N=10^4$) that score lower
on the test set, $0.7526$ against $0.7721$ at $N=10^4$. \QSVM{} is then significantly ahead at
$N=5000$ ($+0.0322$ $[+0.0243,+0.0402]$) and $N=10^4$ ($+0.0327$ $[+0.0180,+0.0475]$, both
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0059$). Choosing the setting with the best mean cross-validated score
gives the same pattern. As with $C$, the setting preferred on the training partition is not
the one that scores best on KDDTest$^+$. At large $N$ the default $\gamma$ of the main
results is therefore the more favourable choice for SVM-RBF; at small $N$ it is the less
favourable one.

\emph{Class prior of the training pool.} We repeated the sweep with the rare classes (R2L and
U2R) enriched twelvefold to $10\%$ of each subset (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning-curve}b,~d). This pool
cannot be sampled beyond $N=2000$: at $N=5000$ the ten runs would share $59\%$ of their rare
records. The enriched arm therefore stops one step before the interval in which the sign changes
under the natural prior, and the design cannot tell whether enrichment removes the reversal. On
the five sizes both arms share ($N=100$ to $2000$), the natural arm finds the tree ensembles
significantly ahead in $8$ of $10$ cells (all except $N=2000$). In the enriched arm the
difference against both ensembles has the same negative sign in $9$ of $10$ cells, but none
reaches significance. Over all $30$ shared cells the sign agrees in $22$, and the enriched arm
reaches a verdict in $4$ cells against $12$ for the natural arm. A sweep restricted to
$N\leq2000$ under either prior would not have reached the sizes at which \QSVM{} ranks first.

\emph{Full-feature reference.} Table~\ref{tab:ref-arm} adds XGBoost and a random forest trained
on all $122$ one-hot features, on the same subsets and seeds, outside the paired protocol.
Full-feature XGBoost peaks at $\refArmXgbPeak$ at $N=\refArmXgbPeakN$ and reaches
$\refArmXgbTenK$ at $N=10^4$. Against it, the difference of \QSVM{} is $\refArmDeltaXgbFiveK$ at
$N=5000$ and $\refArmDeltaXgbTenK$ at $N=10^4$. Both intervals contain zero, so no significant
difference was detected; the design has no equivalence margin and cannot show that the two
models perform equally. Against the full-feature random forest, the interval excludes zero at
$N=5000$ ($+0.0112$ $[+0.0052,+0.0171]$) and $N=10^4$ ($\refArmDeltaRfTenK$). Trained on the whole $125{,}973$-record partition, full-feature XGBoost
reaches $0.804$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}), above every four-qubit result in this paper. The
reversal is thus a property of the shared four-component embedding. Given the raw record,
XGBoost is not beaten by the four-qubit kernel at any size we tested.

\subsection{Rare attack classes}\label{sec:res-rare}

The macro average can hide the classes an operator cares most about, so we report the $F_1$
score of the rare classes (R2L and U2R together) separately. At $N=10^4$ under the natural
prior it is SVM-RBF $0.585$, \QSVM{} $0.507$, \QSVMZ{} $0.421$, XGBoost $0.334$, random
forest $0.331$, SVM-poly2 $0.109$ and SVM-linear $0.089$. The quantum kernel detects rare attacks
much better than either tree ensemble ($+0.176$ and $+0.173$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0039$ for
both), a difference the macro average does not show. SVM-RBF has the higher mean, by $0.078$
$[0.014,0.142]$, but with $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.064$ this comparison is inconclusive. Since all
rare records are attacks, this score does not count false alarms on normal traffic; those
enter the macro average.

This result and the enriched-pool sweep of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-n} measure different things. Here
the metric is rare-class $F_1$ at $N=10^4$; there it is macro-$F_1$ at $N\leq2000$, where the
tree ensembles score higher. The two findings are compatible: at small $N$ the ensembles lead on
the overall score, and at large $N$ the RBF and quantum kernels lead on the rare classes.

\subsection{Transfer to UNSW-NB15}\label{sec:res-unsw}

\input{figs/fig_unsw_transfer}

We applied the selection rule to UNSW-NB15 with the same thresholds and constants
(Fig.~\ref{fig:c1-selection}, bottom). It returns $n^{\ast}=6$, with $V=0.9044$,
$\mathrm{KTA}=0.1986$ and $60$ two-qubit gates. Unlike on NSL-KDD, the alignment gate decides
here: the variance gate admits $n=4,\dots,10$, and only $n=6$ comes within $5\%$ of the best
alignment. On ten independent subsets it returns
$n^{\ast}=6$ in $10/10$ cases at tolerance $\varepsilon\in\{0.02,0.05\}$ and in $7/10$ at
$\varepsilon=0.1$, where the other three give $n^{\ast}=5$. This shows that the choice is
stable across subsets. It does not show that $n=6$ is the best width.

We repeated the training-size sweep at $n=4$ and $n=8$ with $K=35$ and the same subsets, and

With $\gamma$ tuned as in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-n}, SVM-RBF rises from $0.6899$ to $0.7230$ at

\subsection{Test records duplicated in the training data}\label{sec:res-dedup}

\input{tables/dedup_sensitivity}

A test record whose raw features equal those of a training record can reward memorisation.
Such records are $2.9\%$ of the NSL-KDD test set and $25.3\%$ of the UNSW-NB15 test set
(Table~\ref{tab:dedup}). To check whether they drive the comparisons above, we retrained all
seven models at every $N$ and in every run with their stored hyper-parameters, kept the
per-record predictions, and scored them on the full test set and on the test set with duplicates
removed.

For every model except XGBoost, the retrained models matched the stored results to within one
test record in all $120$ UNSW-NB15 trainings (six sizes, ten runs, tuned and frozen arms) and in
$244$ of the $260$ NSL-KDD trainings. We estimate the number of changed test predictions as
$|\Delta\mathrm{accuracy}|$ times the number of test records, a lower bound since changes in
opposite directions cancel. In ten of the remaining sixteen NSL-KDD trainings (four runs at
$N=10^4$ and one at $N=5000$, in both arms) the original training subset could not be rebuilt
exactly; in the other six, one model differs on two to four of the $22{,}544$ test records. The
original runs were made on a different machine, and XGBoost with row subsampling
($\texttt{subsample}<1$) was not reproduced on this one. Its predictions differ in $185$ of the
$188$ NSL-KDD trainings that use subsampling and have a rebuilt subset (median $41$, at most
$313$ records) and in all $102$ UNSW-NB15 trainings that use it (median $70$, at most $471$).
Without row subsampling it was reproduced exactly ($62$ and $18$ trainings). In every case both
sides of the comparison use the retrained models.

Removing duplicates reversed the direction of no cell (Table~\ref{tab:dedup}). Four of the
$108$ cells changed verdict, all of them between significant and inconclusive and all with
Holm-adjusted $p$ close to $0.05$ before removal. On NSL-KDD under the natural prior, the
comparison with the random forest at $N=5000$ became inconclusive ($+0.0080$,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.049$, to $+0.0062$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.16$). In the original runs this
cell was already inconclusive ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.064$), so the natural-prior counts after
removal equal those of the regime map in Sec.~\ref{sec:regime}. In the enriched arm the
comparison with \QSVMZ{} at $N=1000$ became significant in favour of the control
($-0.0234$ $[-0.043,-0.003]$, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.037$). On UNSW-NB15 two cells at $N=5000$
became inconclusive: against SVM-poly2 ($+0.038$ $[-0.001,+0.078]$) and against XGBoost
($-0.015$ $[-0.030,+0.001]$), both at $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.064$. The main comparative findings
hold on the de-duplicated test sets. On NSL-KDD, on the pre-set grid, \QSVM{} remains
significantly ahead of XGBoost at $N=5000$ and $N=10^4$ and of the random forest at $N=10^4$. On UNSW-NB15 the difference
against both ensembles stays negative at every $N$.

\subsection{Circuit width and Gram-matrix concentration}\label{sec:res-width}

\input{figs/fig_width}

Sec.~\ref{sec:res-embed} showed that a larger feature budget requires a wider circuit. We
repeated the NSL-KDD training-size sweep at $K=80$, where the rule returns $n^{\ast}=8$. All $48$
paired comparisons (six baselines, eight training sizes) favour the baseline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:width}a). The entanglement ablation also changes sign: at eight qubits \QSVM{}
scores below its \Zmap{} control at every size, by $0.082$ to $0.171$ macro-$F_1$.

We measured how the Gram matrix changes with width. Let $s(n)$ be the standard deviation of
its off-diagonal entries. Fitting $s(n)\propto n^{-\alpha}$ over $n=4,\dots,10$ at $K=20$ gives
$\alpha_{\ZZ}=0.590$ and $\alpha_{\Zmap}=0.292$, a ratio of $2.02$. A count of phase terms
explains the direction: the \ZZ{} map has $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise terms besides the $n$
single-qubit terms of the \Zmap{} map, so the number of phases it accumulates grows
quadratically rather than linearly in $n$. At $K=80$ the fitted exponents are $0.75$ and
$0.23$, a ratio of $3.32$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:width}b), and over the same range the \ZZ{} kernel loses
$49\%$ of its off-diagonal dispersion against $18\%$ for the \Zmap{} kernel. The count predicts
which map concentrates faster at both budgets, but not the size of the ratio.

Across the seven widths, off-diagonal dispersion and macro-$F_1$ are correlated with $r=+0.77$
($95\%$ CI $[+0.04,+0.96]$, $p=0.04$) for the \ZZ{} kernel and $r=+0.32$ ($[-0.57,+0.87]$,
$p=0.48$) for the \Zmap{} kernel, which concentrates little (Fig.~\ref{fig:width}c). The first
interval only just excludes zero. The difference between the two correlations is not
significant ($z=0.97$, $p=0.33$), so the data do not show that the association is specific to
the concentrating map. These are observational results over seven widths: lower dispersion
goes with lower macro-$F_1$, and we have not shown that one causes the other. Both quantities
change with $n$, so the correlation may reflect their common dependence on width. We fitted
only a power law and compared no other form. The observations are
compatible with the kernel concentration analysed by Thanasilp \emph{et al.}
\cite{thanasilp2024}, who prove that kernel values concentrate exponentially in the number of
qubits under conditions that include high expressivity, entanglement and noise.

Cirillo \emph{et al.} \cite{qmi2026}, studying quantum classifiers for intrusion detection on
ToN\_IoT under noise models derived from IBM backends, report the best balance between
performance and cost at $20$ to $35$ CNOT gates. Our four-qubit operating point uses $24$
two-qubit gates, inside that range, and the degradation we observe on NSL-KDD appears at $112$.
Their results include backend noise. Our width results come from exact, noiseless simulation, so
on NSL-KDD the degradation occurs without any gate error. We did not test
lower-noise hardware, other feature maps or bandwidth scaling \cite{shaydulin2022}, and we draw no conclusion about
whether such changes would extend the useful range of widths.

\subsection{Simulated hardware noise}\label{sec:res-noise}

We checked the operating point under one backend-derived noise model. In each of the ten runs,
both maps were trained on a stratified subset of $200$ training records, with the
regularisation constant selected for the main runs ($C=3$ for both), and scored on the
$300$-record test split. We computed each Gram matrix three ways: by exact statevector
simulation, by sampling with $512$ shots per kernel entry without noise, and by sampling with
$512$ shots under the noise model of \texttt{FakeManilaV2} (gate, readout and
gate-duration-dependent relaxation errors). For the noisy case we transpiled the circuits to
the backend's coupling map, which gives the \ZZ{} feature-map circuit $U_{\Phi}(\bm{x})$ $44$
CNOT gates and depth $59$; the fidelity circuit composes two such circuits. The
smaller training subset explains why the statevector scores below differ from those of
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-ent}.

The mean macro-$F_1$ of \QSVM{} is $0.8329$ under statevector simulation, $0.8363$ with finite
shots and $0.8501$ with noise; for \QSVMZ{} it is $0.8309$, $0.8323$ and $0.8307$.
Relative to statevector simulation, \QSVM{} changes by $+0.0172$ $[+0.0021,+0.0324]$
($p=0.027$, $d_z=0.81$) under noise and \QSVMZ{} by $-0.0002$ $[-0.0115,+0.0111]$. We do
not count the first as an improvement: $p=0.027$ is above the first Holm threshold of any family
this check could be assigned to, from the two noise-versus-ideal comparisons ($0.05/2$) to all
nine comparisons it contains ($0.05/9$).

The Gram matrix changes more than the score. The relative Frobenius distance between the noisy
and the exact Gram matrix averages $0.602$ for \QSVM{} and $0.165$ for \QSVMZ{}, and
alignment falls from $0.194$ to $0.149$ for \QSVM{} and from $0.070$ to $0.068$ for the control.
Under this one noise model, at this circuit depth and with $512$ shots, we detected no loss of
macro-$F_1$. The check covers a single simulated backend and does not establish how the kernel
would behave on physical hardware or under other noise models.
