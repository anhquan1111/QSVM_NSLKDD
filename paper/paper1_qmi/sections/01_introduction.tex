\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Network intrusion detection is an attractive target for quantum machine learning. The data
are tabular and of moderate dimension, the decision is a classification, and a small gain in
detection has operational value \cite{ref24}. Classical machine learning and deep learning are
well established on the task \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3}, which sets the level a quantum method has to
reach. Quantum kernel methods fit the task well: a feature map prepares a state whose
fidelities form a positive semi-definite kernel, and that kernel goes into a standard support
vector machine \cite{havlicek2019,schuld2019}. The quantum part is then a single block inside a
classical pipeline, and it can be compared with classical learners on equal terms.

Studies that apply quantum kernels and variational classifiers to NSL-KDD \cite{ref4} and later
corpora \cite{ref25} report results comparable to, and sometimes above, classical baselines
\cite{ref13,ref14,ref15}. A recent survey \cite{kaissar2026} notes that most of these studies run
in simulation on older datasets and that evaluation practice differs from study to study. Most
evaluate at a single operating point: one qubit count, one training-set size, one class prior.
We find that this choice can decide the result. With the embedding fixed at four principal
components and only the training-set size varied, the ranking of a quantum kernel against
tree ensembles and against linear and polynomial kernels reverses. A benchmark run at one size
can report either an advantage or a deficit.

This paper is an empirical benchmark. We introduce no new kernel or algorithm and do not claim
a quantum advantage. We ask under which conditions a four-qubit \ZZ{} kernel SVM differs
significantly from classical learners given the same input, and we report the conditions
where it does not. The protocol varies one factor at a time. The circuit width is fixed by a
rule that charges for two-qubit gates, before any classifier is trained. The entangling layer
is compared with an otherwise identical circuit without it. The test distribution is shifted
in four ways. The training-set size is swept from $10^2$ to $10^4$ records under the natural
class prior and under a pool enriched in rare attacks. Every comparison is paired over ten
runs, quantum and classical models are tuned by cross-validation on the same subsets, $p$-values are adjusted
for multiple comparisons, and scores are computed on the official test sets, with and without
test records that duplicate training records. UNSW-NB15 serves as a second dataset.

The results form a map of verdicts. In the $42$ comparisons of the training-set-size sweep
under the natural prior, $11$ favour the quantum kernel, $9$ favour a classical baseline and
$22$ are inconclusive; over all $109$ controlled comparisons the counts are $20$, $21$ and $68$.
Under a Benjamini--Hochberg correction over all $109$, three classical verdicts become
inconclusive and every verdict favouring the quantum kernel remains. Within the map:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The ranking reverses with training-set size.} Under the natural prior, on the
    shared four-component embedding, \QSVM{} is next to last at $N=100$ and first at $N=10^4$.
    Its difference from XGBoost changes sign between $N=2000$ and $N=5000$ whether
    hyper-parameters are re-tuned at each size or held fixed, and is significant at $N=10^4$.
    At $N=5000$ it is significant on the pre-set grid of the regularisation constant $C$ but
    not when that grid is widened. On the pre-set grid no size under the natural prior gives a
    significant difference against SVM-RBF. Against XGBoost given all $122$ features, no significant difference was
    detected at $N\ge5000$. The reversal remains after duplicated test records are removed. It
    does not appear on UNSW-NB15. The rare-enriched pool cannot be sampled beyond $N=2000$, so
    the design cannot tell whether enrichment removes the reversal; on the sizes both pools
    share, the enriched arm reaches a verdict in $4$ of $30$ comparisons and the natural arm in
    $12$.
  \item \textbf{The width rule is stable on a second dataset.} The rule returns $n^{\ast}=4$ on NSL-KDD and, with the
    same thresholds, $n^{\ast}=6$ on UNSW-NB15. On ten independent subsets of UNSW-NB15 it
    returns $n^{\ast}=6$ in $10/10$ cases at tolerance $\varepsilon\le0.05$ and in $7/10$ at
    $\varepsilon=0.1$.
  \item \textbf{Wider circuits do worse in noiseless simulation.} A larger feature budget makes
    the rule choose a wider circuit. At $K=80$, where it returns $n^{\ast}=8$, all $48$ paired
    comparisons favour the baseline, including the \Zmap{} control. Across widths, the off-diagonal entries of the
    \ZZ{} Gram matrix become less dispersed faster than those of the control, and lower
    dispersion goes with lower macro-$F_1$ ($r=+0.77$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.04,+0.96]$, seven
    widths). This association is observational and compatible with known kernel
    concentration \cite{thanasilp2024}.
\end{itemize}

The method also loses in several conditions. When the test data are $70\%$ attacks, XGBoost is
significantly ahead of it, and it is the least robust of the seven models to feature
perturbation. Its $F_1$ on the rare attack classes at $N=10^4$ ($0.507$) is well above
that of the tree ensembles ($0.33$); an RBF-kernel SVM has a higher mean ($0.585$), but that
difference is inconclusive.
