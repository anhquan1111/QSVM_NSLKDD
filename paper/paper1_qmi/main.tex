\abstract{Quantum kernel methods for intrusion detection are usually evaluated at a single
operating point, which cannot show when they help. We benchmark a four-qubit
quantum kernel support vector machine with a ZZ feature map against an entanglement-free
control and five classical learners for binary intrusion detection on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15.
The protocol varies one factor at a time (circuit width, entanglement, test distribution and
training-set size), tunes every model by the same procedure, pairs each comparison over ten
runs, and scores on the official test sets with and without test records duplicated in the
training data. Under the natural class prior, 11 of the 42 training-size comparisons favour the
quantum kernel, 9 favour a classical baseline and 22 are inconclusive; over all 110 comparisons
the counts are 21, 21 and 68. On NSL-KDD, on a shared four-component embedding, the quantum kernel is behind XGBoost below 2,000 training records and significantly ahead from
5,000. This reversal remains after duplicated test records are removed, but it does not appear
on UNSW-NB15, and against XGBoost trained on all 122 features no significant difference was
detected. A gate-cost rule for choosing the circuit width, set on NSL-KDD, selects six qubits
on UNSW-NB15. In noiseless simulation an eight-qubit circuit is behind the baseline in all 48
comparisons, and lower dispersion of its Gram matrix goes with lower accuracy. The quantum
kernel is less robust than the tree ensembles to shifts in the class prior. All kernels
were computed by classical simulation.}
